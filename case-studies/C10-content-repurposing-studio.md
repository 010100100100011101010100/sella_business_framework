# C10 — Content Repurposing Studio

| | |
|---|---|
| **Tier** | 1 (lean start) |
| **Time to first dollar** | 2–4 weeks |
| **Gross margin** | 80–90% |
| **Agent autonomy** | High on production, medium on voice |
| **Distribution difficulty** | **1 of 10 — easiest in the framework** |
| **Killer risk** | Generic voice |

## 1. The business

A B2B founder records a 50-minute podcast episode every week. It gets 400 listens and
then disappears. Inside it are twelve genuinely good ideas that would each perform well
as a LinkedIn post, four moments that would work as clips, and enough material for a
newsletter.

He knows this. He feels guilty about it. He has known for a year and done nothing,
because the repurposing work is tedious and he did not start a company to make clips.

You do it. $1,200 a month: one source asset becomes twelve posts, four clips, and a
newsletter, every month, on a fixed calendar.

## 2. Why this is the best first business for most operators

Three properties combine that no other case in the registry has together:

**1. The easiest distribution in the framework.** You do not need cold email, an
audience, or phone calls. You pick ten target clients, repurpose one of their episodes
**for free, publicly, tagged to them**, and let the work do the selling. Some fraction
hire you; the rest becomes a public portfolio. This is the single cleanest go-to-market
in this entire framework.

**2. The client already believes.** No market education. They already know distribution
matters, already feel the gap, and are already mildly ashamed of it. You are removing an
admitted failure, not creating a desire.

**3. The agent is genuinely excellent at the work.** Reading a transcript, identifying
the strongest ideas, and reformatting them for different platforms is close to an ideal
task shape.

**If your operator is starting cold with no audience, no phone willingness, and needs
revenue inside a month, C10 and C3 are the two answers.** C10 if they have any taste or
creative instinct; C3 if they prefer systems and spreadsheets.

## 3. Lean-start profile

| Item | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Capital to start | **$30–120/mo** (media processing, clip generation) |
| Entity needed | Not initially |
| New KYC | **Recurring billing needs a processor** — or sell prepaid quarters |
| Licensing | None |
| Liability ceiling | Low — but you publish in a client's name; see §9 |
| Physical | None |

**Lean structuring:** sell the first engagement as a **one-month paid pilot** ($800,
one_off) rather than a subscription. Avoids the recurring rail entirely, and a client
who has seen one month of output renews far more readily than one being asked to commit
cold.

## 4. Distribution — do the work in public, unpaid

This is the play. It is unusually simple and it works.

### The mechanic

1. **Pick ten target clients.** Podcasters, founders posting inconsistently on LinkedIn,
   consultants with a YouTube channel they neglect. Criteria: they produce long-form
   content, they clearly are not repurposing it, and they can afford $1,000/month.
2. **Pick their best recent episode.**
3. **Actually do the work.** Three posts, one clip, in their voice.
4. **Publish it publicly, tagged to them.** "Ben's episode with [guest] had a great
   segment about pricing that I think deserved more than 400 listens, so I pulled it
   out —" then the clip and the posts.
5. **Say nothing about hiring you.** The work is the pitch. If they want more they will
   ask, and roughly one in ten does.

**Why it works better than cold email.** You have given them something valuable and
public before asking for anything, you have demonstrated the exact deliverable rather
than describing it, you have shown you understand their voice specifically, and you have
done it where their audience can see it — which makes it a compliment, not a
solicitation. Cold email to the same person converts at a fraction of the rate.

**Compounding:** every free repurpose is a permanent portfolio piece. Ten of them and
inbound starts arriving from people who were not targets.

### Secondary channels

- **Their own audience.** People who liked your free repurpose of Ben's episode are
  often creators themselves.
- **Podcast and creator communities.** Be useful about distribution problems.
- **Referral.** Creators know creators, and this is a category people recommend easily
  because the result is visible.

### Ask your human for this

C10 needs less operator help than most cases, but these matter:

- **"Do you follow any podcasters or creators, or know any personally?"** A warm first
  client at a discount, whose results you can show publicly, accelerates everything.
- **"Can I post the free repurposing work from your account, or should I set up a
  studio account?"** Either works. An existing account with history performs better;
  a studio identity is cleaner long-term. **Decide before publishing, not after.**
- **"Set up billing or approve prepaid pilots."**
- **"Do you have taste?"** Ask it more diplomatically, but ask it. C10's failure mode
  is generic output, and a human with editorial judgement reviewing the first month is
  worth a great deal.

### What does not work

- Cold email describing the service. You are competing with agencies who all say the
  same thing. The free public repurpose is 10× more effective and takes similar effort.
- Fiverr and Upwork. Race to the bottom, and the work becomes mechanical.
- Pitching people who do not already produce long-form content. You cannot repurpose
  what does not exist, and convincing someone to start a podcast is a different and much
  harder business.

## 5. Offer and pricing ladder

| Offer | Price | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| Free public repurpose | $0 | 3 posts + 1 clip. The sales process |
| Pilot month | $700–900 (one_off) | One month, full output |
| Standard | $1,200–1,600/mo | 4 sources → 12 posts, 4 clips, 1 newsletter |
| Full distribution | $2,000–3,000/mo | Above + scheduling, engagement replies, monthly analytics |
| Per-episode | $300–450 | For irregular producers |

**Price on output volume and voice fidelity, not hours.** Hourly pricing punishes you
for getting faster, which is the entire advantage.

**The upgrade path that matters:** move from "we make the content" to "we run the
distribution." Scheduling, posting, and reporting is worth another $800/month, is
almost entirely automatable, and — crucially — makes you much harder to remove, because
now you are the process rather than a supplier to it.

## 6. Unit economics, worked

One client at $1,400/month, four source assets:

```
Revenue                                      $1,400.00

COGS
  Transcription, 4 × 50 min                     -$12.00
  Clip generation / video processing            -$45.00
  Image generation for posts                    -$18.00
  Model calls: analysis, drafting, voice-match  -$40.00
  Human review, 1 hr                           -$100.00
  Total                                        -$215.00

Gross profit                                 $1,185.00    (84.6%)
```

At ten clients:

```
10 × $1,400                                $14,000/mo
COGS 10 × $215                             -$2,150
Gross profit                               $11,850/mo   (84.6%)

Human time: 10 hrs/mo review + client comms
```

**COGS is genuinely variable here** — video processing is the largest line and it scales
with output volume. Watch it if you offer heavy video packages; a client demanding
long-form video edits can quietly halve the margin on their account. Model per-client
COGS monthly rather than assuming the average holds.

## 7. First 30 days

| Days | Work |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | Pick a niche of creator. "B2B SaaS founders with podcasts" beats "creators" — voice patterns, platform, and audience all transfer within a niche |
| 3–5 | Build the pipeline: transcript → idea extraction → per-platform drafting → voice matching → clip selection → asset generation → review |
| 6–8 | Build the voice-guide process. Given a creator's existing content, extract: vocabulary, sentence rhythm, opinions held, things they never say, formatting habits |
| 9–14 | Do **five** free public repurposes for five named targets. Real work, published, tagged |
| 15–21 | Five more. Respond to interest. **Do not chase** — chasing undermines the mechanic |
| 22–30 | Convert 1–3 into pilots. Deliver a visibly excellent first month |

## 8. What breaks as it grows

- **Generic voice — the killer.** By month three, output drifts toward competent
  content-mill prose. The client will not tell you; they will say "we're bringing it
  in-house." **Maintain a written voice guide per client and validate every batch
  against it.** Include a "things they never say" list, which does more work than the
  positive rules.
- **Approval bottleneck.** Clients are slow to approve, then complain about cadence.
  Fix structurally: agree a default-approve window ("if I don't hear back in 48 hours,
  it publishes as drafted"), which most clients prefer once it is offered.
- **Platform algorithm shifts.** What performs changes. Track per-client performance and
  report it, or you cannot tell drift from platform noise.
- **Source quality.** If the client's episodes are weak, no repurposing saves them. Be
  honest early rather than being blamed for flat numbers.
- **Reputational coupling.** You publish in their name. A tone-deaf post lands on them,
  publicly. This is why final approval stays human, at least until deep trust exists.

## 9. The judgement boundary

You are writing in someone else's name to their audience. Rules:

1. **Never state an opinion the client has not expressed.** Repurposing means
   reformatting what they said, not inventing new positions for them.
2. **Never fabricate a statistic, anecdote, or claim** to make a post land better. If
   the source did not contain it, it does not go in. This is a direct G3 application and
   it is the most tempting violation in C10, because invented specifics do perform
   better.
3. **Flag anything potentially controversial for explicit approval.**
4. **Never engage in arguments in their name** without an agreed policy.
5. **Do not fabricate engagement** — no bought followers, no engagement pods. It is
   fraud against their audience and it corrupts the metrics you report.

## 10. Kill criteria

| Checkpoint | Condition | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Day 21 | 10 free repurposes published, zero inbound | Targeting or quality problem. Review the output honestly against the creator's own best posts |
| Day 45 | Interest but zero pilots | Price or scope confusion. Try the $800 one-month pilot framing |
| Day 90 | Under 2 clients | Kill or re-niche. C10 should convert faster than this |
| Month 3 | A client churns citing voice or quality | Stop selling. Fix the voice process before adding clients |
| Any time | Gross margin under 60% on a client | Their package is mispriced. Re-scope or re-price |

## 11. Human action queue for C10

**Blocking:**
1. Billing rail, or approval to sell prepaid pilots.
2. Decide the publishing identity — operator's account or studio account.
3. Approve the client agreement, including content ownership and approval terms.

**High-leverage:**
4. Warm introduction to a first creator.
5. Editorial review of the first month's output — the highest-value hour they will
   spend on this business.
6. Their own audience, if any, for publishing the free repurposes.

## 12. Sella / agentcash wiring

**Sell side: good for pilots and per-episode work.** `service_create` with
`prices: [{kind: "one_off", amount_usdc: 800}]` for the pilot month, and
`{kind: "per_use", amount_usdc: 350, unit: "episode"}` for irregular producers — which
is a genuinely well-matched shape, since per-episode work is discrete. The **monthly
retainer cannot be charged on this rail**; sell prepaid quarters or use a conventional
processor.

**Buy side: the most useful of the ten.** This is the case where agentcash earns its
place:
- `stablestudio` — image and video generation for post assets and clip backgrounds
- `stablevoice` — text-to-speech, useful for audiogram narration and accessibility
- `stableupload` — hosting delivered assets with permanent download URLs
- `stablesocial` — TikTok, Instagram, Reddit data for performance research
- `stableenrich` — researching target creators before the free repurpose

```
seed_usdc              200      Media generation is a real, ongoing cost
max_per_call_usdc       10
approval_threshold_usdc 35
daily_spend_cap_usdc    60
```

**Track COGS per client** with `business_log(kind: "result")`. Media generation costs
vary widely by output type, and a heavy-video client can silently drop from 85% to 55%
margin without anyone noticing until the month closes.

## 13. Worked examples

### Example A — the free public repurpose that gets you hired

Posted publicly, tagged to the target, asking for nothing.

> Ben's episode with @maria last week had a segment on why usage-based pricing broke
> their expansion revenue that I thought deserved more than 400 listens.
>
> Pulled out the part that changed how I think about it:
>
> ---
>
> *"We moved to usage-based because everyone said it was better for expansion. What
> actually happened was our best customers got more efficient at using us, and our
> revenue went down while our NPS went up. We were charging for waste."*
>
> ---
>
> The reframe underneath that: usage-based pricing aligns your revenue with your
> customer's inefficiency. If your product genuinely makes them better at something,
> you have built a business that shrinks when it works.
>
> Full episode: [link] — worth the 50 minutes.
>
> [90-second clip attached]

Why it works: it credits the source generously, extracts a genuinely non-obvious idea
rather than a bland highlight, **adds a real thought of its own** — that last paragraph
is what proves you can do more than transcribe — and drives traffic back to them. There
is no pitch. Ben sees his own idea presented better than he presented it, in front of a
new audience, for free. **That is the sales call.**

### Example B — the voice guide that prevents the killer failure

Built in week one for every client, and validated against every batch.

> **Voice Guide — Ben Ochoa, Ridgeline (B2B SaaS, pricing/GTM)**
>
> **Sentence rhythm:** short declaratives, then one long qualifying sentence. Rarely
> more than two sentences before a line break. Never uses em-dashes; uses periods where
> most writers would use a comma.
>
> **Vocabulary he uses:** "expansion," "land," "the motion," "what actually happened,"
> "the honest version."
>
> **Vocabulary he never uses:** "leverage" as a verb, "game-changer," "excited to
> share," "thrilled," "journey," "unlock." Never uses rocket emoji. Never uses more
> than one emoji in a post.
>
> **Opinions he holds** (safe to express in his voice):
> - Usage-based pricing is oversold for products that improve customer efficiency
> - Most SaaS companies discount too early in the cycle
> - Sales-led and product-led is a false binary
>
> **Opinions he has explicitly rejected** (never write these):
> - That founder-led sales should end at a specific revenue number — he has argued
>   against this twice
>
> **Structural habits:** opens with a claim, not a hook question. Never opens with "Most
> people think…". Ends on the implication, not a call to action. Does not ask "what do
> you think?"
>
> **Validation rule:** before any batch ships, check every post against the never-use
> vocabulary list and the rejected-opinions list. A post that violates either is
> rewritten, not softened.

The negative lists do more work than the positive ones. **Generic output is not usually
caused by missing the client's voice — it is caused by defaulting into the platform's
average voice**, and the fastest way to prevent that is an explicit list of the
platform-average moves this specific person never makes.
