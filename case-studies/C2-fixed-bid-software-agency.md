# C2 — Boutique Fixed-Bid Software Agency

| | |
|---|---|
| **Tier** | 2 (lean, but the sale needs a human) |
| **Time to first dollar** | 2–6 weeks |
| **Gross margin** | 55–70% when scoped tightly; **negative when not** |
| **Agent autonomy** | Medium — full build autonomy, human owns the relationship |
| **Distribution difficulty** | 4 of 10 |
| **Killer risk** | Scope creep, then client concentration |

---

## 1. The business

A 30-person company runs something important on a spreadsheet and three Zapier zaps.
It breaks weekly, one person understands it, and that person is now a bottleneck. They
would pay $12,000 to make it a real internal tool, and they cannot get their engineering
team to prioritize it because it is not the product.

You build it. Fixed price, fixed scope, three to six weeks, then you leave.

Highest revenue per unit of the ten. Also the highest variance, because the difference
between a 65% margin and a 10% margin is entirely in how the scope was written.

## 2. Operator fit

**Pick C2 if:** your human will take a 45-minute scoping call and sign a contract, and
ideally has some technical credibility — a GitHub, a past engineering role, a company
they can name. The buyer is spending real money and will want to know who is
accountable.

**Do not pick C2 if:** your operator wants a purely hands-off business. This is not
one. It is the highest-touch case in Tier 2 and the relationship cannot be delegated to
an agent, because the client is buying confidence as much as software.

**The structural thing to understand.** C2 is not a scalable business and should not be
sold as one. It is a **cash engine**. Two projects a quarter at $12k funds everything
else the operator wants to do. Many good operators run C2 alongside C1 or C10 —
agency cash paying for the compounding asset. That is a deliberate and sensible
portfolio, and worth proposing explicitly.

## 3. Lean-start profile

| Item | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Capital to start | **$0–100** |
| Entity needed | **Recommended before the first contract.** A $12k liability against a personal name is a real exposure |
| New KYC | None if the human invoices from an existing account |
| Licensing | None |
| Liability ceiling | **Uncapped unless you cap it.** Cap it at fees paid, in writing, every time |
| Physical | None |

This is the one Tier-2 case where I would not defer the entity. At $500 pilots, personal
liability is theoretical. At $12,000 builds touching a client's operational systems, it
is not. If the operator will not form an entity, at minimum: liability capped at fees
paid, no warranty beyond the acceptance criteria, and no access to production systems
you do not need.

## 4. Distribution

**The channel is outbound for the first three projects and referral thereafter.** By
month four, referral should be the majority of pipeline. If it is not, the delivery
quality is the problem, not the marketing.

### The trigger signals that identify a ready buyer

- **Hiring for an internal-tools or ops-engineering role** — they have admitted the
  need and the budget, and hiring takes four months. You take four weeks.
- **A job posting listing five or more SaaS tools** in an ops role — integration mess
  implied.
- **Recent funding**, 3–9 months after the announcement — the money has landed and the
  scaling pain has arrived.
- **Public complaints about a manual process** — a support forum, a LinkedIn post, a
  podcast where the ops lead describes the spreadsheet.

### The play

Do not pitch capability. Pitch **a specific named process you have inferred is broken**,
with a bounded price and a bounded timeline. "I build internal tools" is noise.
"You're hiring an ops engineer to fix the order-reconciliation process; I can build
that specific thing in four weeks for $11,000" is a conversation.

### Ask your human for this

- **"Will you take the scoping call?"** Blocking. If no, C2 is unavailable.
- **"Can I use your LinkedIn to post two case studies after each project?"** C2's
  referral engine runs on visible proof. A finished project written up publicly
  generates inbound for a year.
- **"Who have you worked with before who might need this, or know someone who does?"**
  The first C2 project should ideally come from the operator's network, not cold. It is
  faster, it converts higher, and a friendly first client is far more forgiving of a
  new process.
- **"Do you have a professional email domain and a place to put a portfolio?"** See
  `10-resources/ref-website.md` — C2 needs a proof site more than most cases.

### What does not work

- Upwork and Fiverr. You will compete with $8/hour and the scoping discipline that
  makes C2 profitable is impossible there.
- Undifferentiated "software agency" positioning. Every agency says it. Pick a narrow
  process type — internal tools, data pipelines, Shopify backends — and own it.

## 5. Offer and pricing ladder

| Offer | Price | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Paid discovery | $1,500–2,500 | 1 week: requirements, acceptance criteria, architecture, fixed quote |
| Small build | $6,000–9,000 | 3 weeks |
| Standard build | $10,000–18,000 | 4–6 weeks |
| Support retainer | $800–2,000/mo | Post-launch fixes and small changes |

**Sell the paid discovery separately, always.** This is the most important commercial
mechanic in C2 and the one most often skipped. It does four things: it gets money
moving early, it qualifies out the unserious, it makes the fixed quote *accurate*
because you scoped before you priced, and it means an unwinnable project still paid.
Free scoping is how agencies lose two weeks a month.

**Payment terms are non-negotiable: 50% on signature, 50% on acceptance.** Never start
on a promise. For projects above $15k, use three milestones. A client who will not pay
a deposit will not pay the balance.

## 6. Unit economics, worked

A $12,000 build quoted at four weeks.

```
Revenue                                    $12,000

Scoped correctly (4 weeks actual)
  Agent build time                              n/a  (your time is the input)
  Human time: 6 calls + contract ≈ 8 hrs   -$800     at $100/hr opportunity cost
  Infrastructure, APIs, services            -$300
  Handover docs and training                -$200
  Gross profit                            $10,700    = 89% before operator time is
                                                       fully loaded
Loaded against 4 weeks of capacity                   ≈ 65% effective margin
```

Now the same project with scope creep — six additional "small" requests accepted
without a change order, pushing delivery to seven weeks:

```
Revenue                                    $12,000    (unchanged — that is the trap)
Duration                                    7 weeks   (+75%)
Effective margin                                     ≈ 20–30%
Opportunity cost: the next $12k project you could not start
```

**The scope did not become unprofitable through one big change. It became unprofitable
through six small ones, each individually reasonable to refuse and individually
awkward to refuse.** This is why C2 uses a V-model: the acceptance criteria are signed
before the build, so "is this in scope?" has a documented answer rather than a social
negotiation.

**The change-order script, which you should use verbatim:**

> That's a good addition and I'd like to include it. It's outside the signed acceptance
> criteria, so it's a change order — about $1,400 and three days, and it moves delivery
> to the 22nd. Want me to write it up?

Never refuse. Always price. This converts a margin leak into revenue and it makes
clients dramatically more thoughtful about what they ask for.

## 7. Delivery model — why V-model, not agile

This is the framework's clearest example of **contract shape forcing methodology**.

A fixed price with an open scope is an unbounded liability. Agile inside a fixed bid,
with no change-order mechanism, is how agencies deliver excellent work and lose money
doing it. The V-model applies here because every requirement must have a matching
acceptance test agreed *before* implementation:

```
Requirements  ──────────────────────►  Acceptance testing
  (signed by client)                     (against the signed list)
    │                                          ▲
    ▼                                          │
  Design       ──────────────────────►  Integration testing
    │                                          ▲
    ▼                                          │
  Build        ──────────────────────►  Unit testing
```

Left side descends before any code; right side ascends against artifacts written on
the left. "Done" is not an opinion — it is the signed list, satisfied.

**Iterate *within* the scope, not on the scope.** You can and should show working
software weekly, take feedback on implementation, and adjust the how. The what is
fixed at signature. See `00-core/delivery-model-selector.md`.

## 8. What breaks as it grows

- **Client concentration.** At three clients, one is 60% of revenue. When they finish,
  revenue drops by 60% with no warning. Guardrail G5 exists for this case: no client
  above 40% of trailing-90-day revenue after month three, and the way you fix it is by
  keeping outbound running *during* delivery, which is exactly when it feels
  unnecessary.
- **The delivery/sales oscillation.** Deliver hard, pipeline empties, scramble to sell,
  deliver hard again. The fix is structural: a fixed weekly outbound block that happens
  regardless of how busy delivery is. Put it in the operating cadence, not in intentions.
- **Support tail.** Every finished project generates questions forever. Either sell the
  retainer or define a 30-day warranty and enforce it.
- **Estimation drift.** You will underestimate for the first three projects.
  Systematically log estimate vs. actual per project and apply the measured multiplier
  to future quotes. Most people need 1.4–1.8× their instinct.

## 9. Kill criteria

| Checkpoint | Condition | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Day 30 | Zero discovery calls booked | Targeting or the trigger signals are wrong |
| Day 60 | Calls but zero paid discovery | The offer is too abstract. Get more specific about the process you fix |
| Day 90 | Zero projects closed | Kill or restructure. C2 should produce revenue faster than this |
| Any project | Actual duration > 1.6× estimate | Stop taking new work. Fix scoping before selling again |
| Month 6 | Referral is under 30% of pipeline | Delivery quality problem. Diagnose before scaling spend |

## 10. Human action queue for C2

**Blocking:**
1. Take the scoping call and be the named accountable person.
2. Sign contracts. Only a legal person can.
3. Set up invoicing and receive payment.
4. Approve the master services agreement, including the liability cap.
5. Form the entity — recommended before the first contract at this deal size.

**High-leverage:**
6. Warm introductions for the first project.
7. LinkedIn presence for case studies after each delivery.
8. Reference calls for later prospects — a past client talking to a future one closes
   deals nothing else closes.

## 11. Sella / agentcash wiring

**Sell side: weak, and do not force it.** A $12,000 B2B contract belongs on an invoice
paid by bank transfer through the human's entity. Do not ask a corporate finance team
to pay in USDC; you will lose the deal over the payment rail. `service_create` is
reasonable for the **paid discovery** at `kind: "one_off"` if the client is
crypto-comfortable, but treat that as the exception.

**Buy side: genuinely useful.** agentcash `stableenrich` for prospect research and
trigger detection — job postings, funding events, tech-stack signals. `search_catalog`
for datasets when a build needs reference data.

**Suggested spend policy:**

```
seed_usdc              150
max_per_call_usdc       10
approval_threshold_usdc 30
daily_spend_cap_usdc    40
```

Log each project's scope, change orders, and estimate-vs-actual with `business_log`.
Your operator should be able to see margin per project without asking for a report.

## 12. Worked examples

### Example A — the outbound email that books discovery calls

> Subject: your ops engineer role
>
> Hi Marcus,
>
> You're hiring an Ops Engineer and the posting mentions reconciling Shopify orders
> against your 3PL's exports. I'm guessing that's manual right now and someone spends a
> day a week on it.
>
> That's a four-week build. I do fixed-price internal tools — I'd scope it properly
> first ($2,000, one week, you get the requirements doc and a firm quote whether or not
> you build with me), then build it for a fixed number.
>
> Hiring for it takes four months. Worth a 20-minute call to see if it's the same shape
> I think it is?
>
> — [name]

Why it works: it reads one specific line of their job posting rather than the whole
thing, names the pain in their language, prices the first step at a level requiring no
committee, makes the discovery valuable *even if they do not hire you*, and closes with
the honest comparison — four months versus four weeks — that reframes the entire
decision. The ask is twenty minutes, not a project.

### Example B — the acceptance criteria that protect the margin

From the paid discovery deliverable. This is the artifact that makes the fixed bid
survivable.

> **AC-04 — Order reconciliation report**
>
> Given a Shopify export and a 3PL export for the same date range, the system produces
> a report listing: matched orders, orders present in Shopify but not the 3PL, orders
> present in the 3PL but not Shopify, and quantity mismatches on matched orders.
>
> **Verified by:** running both exports for 2026-08-01 to 2026-08-31 and confirming the
> four categories total to the Shopify order count.
>
> **Explicitly out of scope:** automatic correction of mismatches; writing back to
> Shopify; historical reconciliation before 2026-01-01; any 3PL other than [named].
>
> _Signed: [client], [date]_

The out-of-scope block is the most valuable text in a C2 engagement. Every line in it
is a conversation you will not have to have in week five, or — more precisely — a
conversation that becomes a priced change order instead of an argument. **Write the
out-of-scope list longer than feels necessary.** Nobody has ever regretted that.
