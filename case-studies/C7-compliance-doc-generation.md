# C7 — SMB Compliance & Policy Document Generation

| | |
|---|---|
| **Tier** | **3 — do not start here. Graduate into it** |
| **Time to first dollar** | 3–6 weeks |
| **Gross margin** | ~90% |
| **Agent autonomy** | Medium — production high, **sign-off must be human** |
| **Distribution difficulty** | 6 of 10 |
| **Killer risk** | Unauthorized practice of law |

---

## 1. Why this is Tier 3

Your operator asked to start lean, with minimal regulatory boundaries. **C7 is the
opposite of that**, and I am putting the warning before the description.

Generating a privacy policy and telling a client it makes them GDPR-compliant is legal
advice. In most jurisdictions, giving legal advice without a licence is a regulatory
offence, and — more practically — it transfers the client's compliance failure onto your
operator's personal liability. A 40-person company that fails an audit because your
document was wrong will look for someone to blame, and their lawyer will find your
operator.

**Prerequisites before C7 is available to you:**

1. An entity, for the liability shield.
2. Professional liability insurance (errors & omissions).
3. Either an attorney partner who reviews and signs off, or unambiguous positioning as
   drafts for the client's own counsel to review.
4. Ideally, existing revenue from a Tier-1 case so C7 is not your only source.

**The one exception:** if your operator is already a qualified lawyer, compliance
professional, or certified auditor, C7 moves to Tier 1 for them — the professional
boundary is already satisfied, and their credential is the differentiator that makes
this business excellent rather than dangerous.

## 2. The business

A 45-person company just got its first enterprise prospect. The prospect sent a
120-question security questionnaire, wants a copy of their information security policy,
their data retention policy, their incident response plan, and asks whether they are
SOC 2 compliant. The company has none of these and the deal is worth $400,000.

You produce the pack. Two to three weeks, $1,800, and they can answer the
questionnaire.

The urgency is genuine and the buyer is highly motivated, which is why the economics
are good. The risk is that you are producing documents whose entire purpose is to be
relied upon.

## 3. Positioning — the sentence that makes this safe

Everything about C7's safety comes down to how it is positioned, consistently, in
every artifact:

> **These are drafts prepared for review by your qualified counsel. They are not legal
> advice and do not themselves establish compliance with any regulation or standard.**

That disclaimer must appear in **the deliverable itself**, not only in the contract.
Clients keep documents for years and forget contracts in weeks.

**Things you must never say, in any channel:**

- "This makes you GDPR compliant" / "SOC 2 compliant"
- "You're covered"
- "This will pass an audit"
- Any characterization of your output as legal advice or an opinion of counsel

**Things you can say honestly:**

- "This is the document set auditors typically ask for."
- "This is drafted against the SOC 2 Trust Services Criteria and maps each policy to
  the criteria it addresses."
- "Your counsel should review this before you rely on it."

The attorney-partner model converts the largest risk into the largest differentiator:
an attorney reviews and signs the pack, you charge $3,500 instead of $1,800, and the
client gets something they can actually rely on. Revenue share with a solo practitioner
is usually available and is worth every point.

## 4. Lean-start profile

| Item | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Capital to start | $200–600 (standards access, insurance quote) |
| Entity needed | **Yes, before the first client** |
| New KYC | Entity formation, business banking, insurance underwriting |
| Licensing | None *if positioned as drafts*. Otherwise, a licence you do not have |
| Liability ceiling | **High.** Cap contractually and carry E&O insurance |
| Physical | None |

This is the highest setup burden in the framework. That is the price of the margin.

## 5. Distribution

### The trigger signals

C7 has unusually crisp buying triggers, which makes outbound effective:

- **Hiring a first security, compliance, or IT role** — they have admitted the gap
- **Announcing an enterprise customer or a move upmarket** — questionnaires are coming
- **A funding round of Series A or later** — investors and customers both start asking
- **Job postings mentioning SOC 2, ISO 27001, or HIPAA** for a company that clearly
  does not have it yet
- **Handling health, financial, or EU personal data** with no visible privacy page
- **A public breach or incident in their sector** — sector-wide urgency

### The play

Send a **gap assessment**, not a pitch. Review what is publicly visible — privacy
policy present or absent, security page, subprocessor list, cookie handling, terms —
and send a one-page list of what an enterprise buyer will ask for and which items they
currently cannot produce.

Same structural mechanic as C3 and C6: do a piece of the real work first. The
difference is that C7's free artifact must itself carry the disclaimer.

### Ask your human for this

- **"Do you know a solo attorney or compliance consultant who would review packs for a
  revenue share?"** The single highest-value question in C7. It changes the risk
  profile, the price, and the credibility simultaneously.
- **"Will you form the entity and get an E&O quote?"** Blocking.
- **"Do you have any compliance, legal, security, or audit background — or a
  certification?"** If yes, that is the marketing. Lead with it.
- **"Can I publish gap-assessment content from your LinkedIn?"** Compliance content
  performs well with this buyer and generates inbound.
- **"Approve the standard disclaimer language."** Blocking.

### What does not work

Competing on price with template mills. There are $99 privacy policy generators. You are
not selling documents; you are selling *the right document set, mapped to what your
buyer's customers will ask for*. If the client wants a $99 template, let them have it —
they are not your buyer.

## 6. Offer and pricing ladder

| Offer | Price | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| Free gap assessment | $0 | One page. Opens the conversation |
| Starter pack | $900–1,400 | Privacy policy, terms, cookie notice, DPA template |
| Enterprise-readiness pack | $1,800–2,800 | Above + infosec policy, retention, incident response, access control, vendor management, security questionnaire answer bank |
| SOC 2 readiness | $3,500–6,000 | Above + control mapping, evidence register, gap remediation plan |
| Maintenance | $250–500/mo | Regulatory updates, questionnaire support, annual review |
| Attorney-reviewed variant | +60–100% | With sign-off from the partner practitioner |

**The security questionnaire answer bank is the most under-priced item on this list and
the most valuable to the client.** Every enterprise deal brings another 120-question
form. A maintained answer bank turns a two-week scramble into an afternoon, and it is
the natural anchor for the maintenance retainer.

## 7. Unit economics, worked

An enterprise-readiness pack at $2,200:

```
Revenue                                        $2,200

COGS
  Standards / framework reference access         -$80
  Research and drafting compute                  -$60
  Human review time (2 hrs)                     -$200
  Attorney review, if used (revenue share)      -$700    optional but recommended
  Total, without attorney                       -$340
  Total, with attorney                        -$1,040

Gross profit without attorney                  $1,860   (85%)
Gross profit with attorney                     $1,160   (53%)
```

**The attorney costs you 32 margin points and is usually still the right call**, because
it (a) transfers the professional liability to someone insured to carry it, (b) lets you
raise the price to $3,500, which restores the margin, and (c) is the only version of
this business you can grow without the risk compounding with volume.

Model it at the higher price and the arithmetic is straightforward:

```
Revenue with attorney review                   $3,500
COGS including attorney share                 -$1,340
Gross profit                                   $2,160   (62%) — and someone else
                                                        carries the professional risk
```

## 8. Delivery model — V-model, and here it is genuinely correct

Compliance artifacts exist to be audited. An auditor asks *"which control does this
clause satisfy?"* and the answer must be a trace, not a narrative.

```
Requirement register  ─────────►  Pack verification
  (which frameworks, which          (every requirement has a
   criteria, which client            document that addresses it)
   specifics)                              ▲
      │                                    │
      ▼                                    │
Document plan         ─────────►  Cross-document consistency check
  (which docs, which                 (retention periods, role names,
   sections)                          and definitions agree everywhere)
      │                                    ▲
      ▼                                    │
Drafting              ─────────►  Clause-level review against register
```

**The verification side is not optional and it is where the value is.** The most common
real defect in compliance packs is *internal inconsistency*: the retention policy says
7 years, the privacy notice says 3, and the questionnaire answer says 5. An auditor
finds that in ten minutes and the client's credibility is gone. Build the consistency
check as an explicit gate.

This is why C7 is the framework's second V-model case alongside C2. See
`00-core/delivery-model-selector.md`.

## 9. What breaks as it grows

- **Regulatory drift.** Standards and laws change. Documents you shipped last year go
  stale. This is a liability if you sold them as current — and it is precisely why the
  maintenance retainer exists. Sell it as a genuine need, because it is one.
- **Scope confusion into advisory.** The client starts asking "should we do X?" That is
  consulting, and depending on phrasing it may be legal advice. Route those questions to
  the attorney partner or decline them explicitly.
- **Template sameness.** If every client's pack is identical, an auditor who has seen
  two of yours will discount both. Client-specific detail is not padding; it is the
  thing that makes the document credible.
- **Questionnaire support becoming unbounded.** Cap it: "up to two questionnaires per
  month" in the retainer.

## 10. Kill criteria

| Checkpoint | Condition | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Before day 1 | No entity, or no E&O quote obtained | **Do not start.** Run a Tier-1 case instead |
| Day 30 | 30 gap assessments sent, zero calls | Trigger signals are wrong |
| Day 60 | Zero clients | Positioning is likely too close to a template mill. Differentiate on the questionnaire bank |
| Any time | A client asserts your document made them compliant and you did not correct it | **Stop and correct it in writing immediately.** This is the failure that ends the business |
| Any time | Asked to give an opinion on a specific legal question | Decline or route to the attorney. Every time |

## 11. Human action queue for C7

**Blocking, all of them:**
1. Form the entity.
2. Obtain E&O / professional liability insurance.
3. Approve the disclaimer language for use in every deliverable.
4. Sign client agreements with the liability cap.
5. Decide the attorney-partner question.

**High-leverage:**
6. Their own compliance/legal/security credential, if any — it is the marketing.
7. Introductions to fractional CTOs, CFOs, and startup lawyers, who see this need
   constantly and refer readily.

## 12. Sella / agentcash wiring

**Sell side: weak.** These are invoiced B2B engagements through the entity. Do not put
compliance work on a crypto rail; the buyer is, by definition, a risk-averse company.

**Buy side: moderate.** Sella `search_catalog` for framework and standards reference
material; agentcash `stableenrich` for trigger detection (job postings, funding, public
security pages).

```
seed_usdc              150
max_per_call_usdc       10
approval_threshold_usdc 30
daily_spend_cap_usdc    40
```

Use `business_log(kind: "milestone")` at each V-model gate — requirement register
signed, drafting complete, consistency check passed, human review passed. In a
liability-bearing business, **the log is your evidence that a process was followed**,
and it is worth maintaining for that reason alone.

## 13. Worked examples

### Example A — the gap assessment that opens the conversation

> Subject: what your enterprise buyer is about to ask for
>
> Hi Tom,
>
> Congratulations on the Series A — that usually means enterprise prospects start
> arriving within a quarter, and with them the security questionnaires.
>
> I looked at what's publicly visible for Arbor and put together the list an enterprise
> buyer's security team will ask for. From the outside:
>
> **You have:** a privacy policy (last updated 2024), terms of service.
>
> **They will ask for, and I can't find:** information security policy, data retention
> schedule, incident response plan, subprocessor list, access control policy, business
> continuity plan, and a completed security questionnaire or SIG Lite.
>
> **The one I'd fix first:** the subprocessor list. It's the fastest to produce and
> it's the first thing a privacy-conscious buyer checks, because its absence suggests
> nothing behind it exists either.
>
> One page attached with the detail. It's a starting point for your counsel, not legal
> advice — but it's the map of what's coming.
>
> If it's useful, the full pack is $2,200 and takes about two weeks.
>
> — [name]

Note the disclaimer appears naturally in the body rather than as a legal footer, the
observations are all about *publicly visible absence* (verifiable, not speculative), and
it gives one prioritized recommendation rather than an overwhelming list. **Never claim
to know what is missing internally — only what is not publicly available.** That
distinction is both honest and defensible.

### Example B — the traceability table that justifies the price

Included as the first page of every pack. This artifact is what separates you from a
template generator.

> **Control Coverage Map — Arbor Systems, prepared 2026-09-03**
>
> | Requirement | Framework ref | Addressed by | Section | Client-specific? |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Logical access provisioning | SOC 2 CC6.1 | Access Control Policy | §3.2 | Yes — Okta + AWS IAM |
> | Access revocation on termination | SOC 2 CC6.2 | Access Control Policy | §3.5 | Yes — 4-hour SLA per your HR process |
> | Data retention schedule | GDPR Art. 5(1)(e) | Data Retention Policy | §2 | Yes — 7 yr financial, 24 mo logs |
> | Breach notification, 72 hours | GDPR Art. 33 | Incident Response Plan | §5.1 | Yes — named contacts |
> | Subprocessor disclosure | GDPR Art. 28(2) | Subprocessor List | full | Yes — 11 vendors identified |
> | Vendor risk assessment | SOC 2 CC9.2 | Vendor Management Policy | §4 | Partially — process defined, register empty |
>
> **Known gaps, stated deliberately:** the vendor risk register is a template with no
> entries. Populating it requires your input on each vendor's data access. Listed here
> so it is not mistaken for complete.
>
> _These documents are drafts prepared for review by qualified counsel. They do not
> themselves establish compliance with any framework or regulation._

The "known gaps, stated deliberately" row is the most important thing on the page.
**Declaring what your work does not cover is what makes the rest of it trustworthy** —
and it is a direct application of guardrail G3. A pack that claims completeness it does
not have is worse than no pack, because it creates false confidence in a company that
is about to be audited.
