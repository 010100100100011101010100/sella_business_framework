# C4 — Vertical Daily News Brief / Paid Newsletter

| | |
|---|---|
| **Tier** | 2 (needs a billing rail; slow to money) |
| **Time to first dollar** | **8–16 weeks — slowest of the ten** |
| **Gross margin** | ~90% |
| **Agent autonomy** | High on production, **low on judgement** |
| **Distribution difficulty** | 9 of 10 |
| **Killer risk** | Aggregation without a point of view |

---

## 1. The business

Every weekday morning, a narrow professional audience gets five things they cannot
afford to have missed in their field — with an opinion attached about what each one
means. Medical device regulatory. Municipal bond issuance. Semiconductor supply chain.
Immigration policy for employment visas. Restaurant industry labour law.

You monetize with sponsorship first and subscriptions second.

## 2. Read this before choosing C4

I am going to be blunt, because this is the case where enthusiasm most consistently
outruns judgement.

**Automated news summarization has approximately zero market value.** Your reader can
already generate a competent summary of any article, free, in five seconds. If your
brief is "here are five things that happened, summarized," you have built something the
market values at nothing, and no amount of design or consistency will fix it.

**What people pay for is a person with a view telling them what it means.** The
sentence that makes a brief valuable is not "the FDA published draft guidance on
software as a medical device." It is "the FDA's draft guidance means your Class II
submission timeline just moved by roughly four months, and if you are mid-submission
you should call your regulatory consultant this week."

That second sentence requires domain judgement. Either your operator has it, or you
must build the brief around synthesis that is genuinely non-obvious — cross-source
patterns, quantitative tracking nobody else maintains, primary-document reading that
others skip.

**Therefore: do not pick C4 unless your operator has real domain expertise in the
niche, or is willing to develop and voice a genuine point of view weekly.** With that,
C4 becomes an extraordinary asset — a compounding audience that makes every subsequent
business easier. Without it, it is 16 weeks of unpaid work ending in a list nobody
reads.

## 3. Operator fit

**Pick C4 if:** the operator has domain expertise, an existing niche audience, or is
already known in a professional community — and can fund a quiet quarter.

**Do not pick C4 if:** the operator needs revenue inside 60 days. This is the wrong
case and C3 or C10 is the right one.

**The strategic argument for C4 despite everything above:** an owned audience of 2,000
engaged professionals in a valuable niche is the single most durable asset in this
framework. It makes C6, C7, C8, and C10 trivially easy to launch later, because you
already have the buyers. Many operators should run C4 *second*, funded by a Tier-1
business, rather than first.

## 4. Lean-start profile

| Item | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Capital to start | **$0–50/mo** — free tiers cover the first 1,000 subscribers |
| Entity needed | Not until sponsorship revenue is regular |
| New KYC | **Required for subscriptions** — Stripe/Substack/Ghost, human's identity |
| Licensing | None — but see defamation and financial-advice notes below |
| Liability ceiling | Low, with two exceptions |
| Physical | None |

**Two liability exceptions worth naming.** If the niche is finance, stating opinions
about specific securities can bring you within investment-advice regulation in several
jurisdictions — avoid recommendations about named instruments. And in any niche,
writing that a named company did something wrong is defamation exposure if it is not
accurate. Report facts with sources; opinion should be recognizably opinion.

## 5. Distribution — the section that decides whether you eat

C4's distribution problem is the second-hardest in the framework: **you must build an
audience before anyone pays you anything.** There is no sample-led shortcut like C3.

### The channels that work, in order

**1. Clips of the brief itself.** The highest-return activity. Take the sharpest single
insight from each issue and post it standalone on the platform where the niche lives —
LinkedIn for most professional verticals, X for finance and tech, a specific subreddit
or forum for others. Not "subscribe to my newsletter." The insight itself, delivered
free, with the brief mentioned at the end. Do this daily. It is the whole growth engine.

**2. The referral loop, built in from issue one.** A simple mechanic: refer three
people, get the quarterly deep-dive report. Newsletters grow through their readers or
they do not grow.

**3. Being genuinely useful in the niche's existing gathering places.** Answer questions
in the industry forum, the LinkedIn group, the Slack community — as a knowledgeable
participant, not a marketer. Slow, unglamorous, and it produces the highest-quality
subscribers you will get.

**4. SEO on the archive, which pays from month four.** Every issue is a page. The niche
searches specific regulatory phrases and product names. This compounds quietly.

**5. Cross-promotion with adjacent newsletters.** Once you have 500 subscribers you
have something to trade. Find non-competing newsletters serving the same reader and
swap mentions.

### Ask your human for this — C4 depends on it more than any other case

**C4 is the case where operator assistance is worth the most, because audience cannot
be manufactured and reputation cannot be delegated.** Ask directly:

- **"What is your actual expertise, and what do you believe about this industry that
  most people in it get wrong?"** That answer is the brief's editorial position. Without
  it you have an aggregator. Get it in writing, in the charter.
- **"Will you put your name and face on this?"** Anonymous professional newsletters
  underperform badly. People subscribe to a person.
- **"Can I draft daily social posts for you to approve and publish from your account?"**
  Their account has history and standing; a new one has neither and will be
  algorithmically throttled.
- **"Which communities, groups, Slacks, and forums are you already in?"** These are
  your first 200 subscribers and they cost nothing.
- **"Who are the 20 most respected people in this niche, and do you know any of them?"**
  One of them sharing issue one is worth three months of cold growth.
- **"Will you review each issue before it sends, at least for the first month?"** The
  editorial voice needs calibration, and a wrong claim in a professional niche is
  reputationally expensive.
- **"Set up the email platform account"** — blocking, and it requires their identity.

## 6. Offer and pricing ladder

| Offer | Price | When |
|---|---|---|
| Free daily brief | $0 | From day one. This is the product |
| Sponsor slot | $400–1,500/issue or /week | **From ~1,000 engaged subscribers** |
| Premium tier | $15–30/mo | From ~3,000 subscribers, not before |
| Quarterly deep-dive report | $150–400 | Anytime — often the fastest first revenue |
| Job board / classifieds | $200–400/post | Once the audience is employable |

**Monetize sponsors before subscriptions.** A sponsor pays $800 for access to 1,200
engaged readers. Getting $800/month from subscriptions at $20/mo requires 40 paying
subscribers, which at a 3–5% conversion rate requires roughly 1,000 *paid-willing*
readers — a much later milestone. Sponsors are the near-term revenue; subscriptions
are the year-two business.

**The billing constraint.** Sella cannot charge subscriptions (x402 settles one request
at a time, no standing authority). A premium tier therefore requires Stripe, Substack,
Ghost, or Beehiiv under the human's identity. **The quarterly report, however, is a
`one_off` and can settle natively** — which is part of why it is often the fastest
first dollar in C4.

## 7. Unit economics, worked

At 1,500 subscribers with one weekly sponsor:

```
Revenue
  Sponsor, 4 issues/mo × $600                  $2,400
  Quarterly report, amortized                    $200
  Total                                        $2,600/mo

COGS
  Email platform (1,500 subs)                    -$49
  Source subscriptions / paywalled trade press   -$120
  Research API calls                              -$40
  Total                                          -$209

Gross profit                                   $2,391/mo   (92%)
```

The economics are excellent **once the audience exists**. Model the path there honestly:

```
Month 1     0 →   150 subscribers    $0 revenue
Month 2   150 →   400                $0
Month 3   400 →   750                $0  (or a first $300 report sale)
Month 4   750 → 1,200                first sponsor, ~$600
Month 5  1,200 → 1,700               ~$1,800
Month 6  1,700 → 2,300               ~$2,400
```

**Show this table to your operator in week one.** Four months of near-zero revenue is
the deal. An operator who agrees to that up front is patient in month three; one who
was not told is gone by month two, and they will be right to be annoyed.

## 8. First 30 days

| Days | Work |
|---|---|
| 1–3 | Lock the niche and the editorial position with the operator. Write the one-sentence promise: *"Every weekday, X for Y, in five minutes."* |
| 4–5 | Set up the email platform (human's account). Landing page with a single promise and one field. |
| 6–7 | Build the source pipeline: primary documents, regulator feeds, trade press, key people. **Primary sources beat news aggregators** — that is where non-obvious findings live. |
| 8–12 | Write five issues before publishing any. This proves you can sustain the cadence and lets the operator calibrate voice. |
| 13 | Launch to the operator's existing network personally — individual messages, not a broadcast. First 50 subscribers. |
| 14–30 | **Publish every single weekday without exception.** Post one clip daily. Reply to every single reply personally. |

**The unforgiving rule of C4: cadence is the product.** A brief that skips days teaches
readers not to depend on it, and dependence is the entire value proposition. If you
cannot sustain daily, publish weekly and be excellent — but never announce a frequency
you will miss.

## 9. What breaks as it grows

- **Voice drift.** Issue 60 sounds like a template. Keep a written voice guide and
  re-read issue 5 quarterly.
- **Source dependency.** If one trade publication is 60% of your inputs, you are their
  unpaid summarizer and you are exposed to their paywall changes. Diversify toward
  primary documents.
- **Sponsor mismatch.** Taking a sponsor your readers distrust costs more in unsubscribes
  than the slot pays. Turn down bad money — and say publicly that you do.
- **The judgement bottleneck.** As volume grows, the temptation to ship without the
  operator's view increases, and quality decays exactly where the value lives.

## 10. Kill criteria

| Checkpoint | Condition | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Day 30 | < 100 subscribers | Niche is too small, or the promise is not specific enough |
| Day 60 | < 300 subscribers, or open rate < 35% | Diagnose: growth problem or content problem. They need different fixes |
| Day 90 | < 600 subscribers | Change niche once, or kill |
| Day 120 | No sponsor interest at 1,000+ subscribers | The audience is not commercially valuable. Reconsider the niche's buyer |
| Any time | Open rate below 25% for three weeks | The content stopped being worth opening. Fix before growing |

**Open rate is your real health metric, not subscriber count.** A 4,000-subscriber list
at 20% is worth less than 900 subscribers at 55%, and sponsors know it.

## 11. Human action queue for C4

**Blocking:**
1. Email platform account (their identity, their KYC).
2. Stripe or equivalent, **if** a paid tier is planned.
3. The editorial position — written down, in their words.
4. Consent to use their name and face.
5. Physical mailing address for CAN-SPAM compliance in the footer of every send.

**High-leverage:**
6. Personal launch messages to their network.
7. Daily social posting from their established account.
8. Introductions to respected people in the niche.
9. Weekly review of issues for the first month.

## 12. Sella / agentcash wiring

**Sell side, partial.** Sponsorship slots work as `service_create` with
`kind: "one_off"` — a sponsor buying a week is a discrete purchase. The quarterly report
is a clean `one_off` too. `storefront_set` makes a serviceable sponsor-facing media kit:
a `metric` block with subscriber count and open rate, a `table` of past issues, an
`offering` block for the slot. **Every number in that media kit must be measured and
current** — inflating audience metrics to a sponsor is fraud, not marketing, and it is
the fastest way to end the business (G3).

**Subscriptions: not possible on this rail.** Conventional processor required.

**Buy side.** agentcash `stableenrich` for web search and article retrieval;
`stablestudio` for social clip imagery; `stableemail` only for outbound sponsor
prospecting, never for the brief itself — subscriber sending belongs on a real ESP with
list management and one-click unsubscribe.

```
seed_usdc              100
max_per_call_usdc        5
approval_threshold_usdc 20
daily_spend_cap_usdc    25
```

## 13. Worked examples

### Example A — aggregation versus judgement, same story

**Worthless (what an unguided agent produces):**

> **FDA publishes draft guidance on AI-enabled device software functions**
> The FDA released draft guidance covering lifecycle management for AI-enabled medical
> device software. Comments are due in 60 days. [link]

**Valuable (what someone pays for):**

> **FDA's AI guidance quietly moves your submission timeline**
> The draft guidance on AI-enabled device software landed Tuesday. The headline is
> lifecycle management, but the operative change is in section 4.2: predetermined change
> control plans now expect performance monitoring data *at submission*, not post-market.
>
> **What it means for you:** if you are mid-submission with an adaptive algorithm and
> planned to commit to monitoring after clearance, that is now a gap. Two device makers
> I spoke with are adding roughly four months.
>
> **What to do this week:** if you have a PCCP in flight, read 4.2 before your next
> regulatory call. Comments close 12 November — this is a genuinely open door and the
> agency has moved on comments in this area before. [link to primary document]

The second reads the primary document rather than the press release, isolates the
non-obvious operative change, states the consequence in the reader's terms, and gives
a dated action. That is the entire difference between a business and a hobby.

### Example B — the sponsor pitch, sent at 1,200 subscribers

> Subject: 1,200 regulatory affairs professionals, weekly
>
> Hi Dana,
>
> I write a daily brief for medical device regulatory professionals — 1,240 subscribers,
> 61% open rate, measured over the last 30 days. Mostly RA/QA managers and directors at
> device manufacturers, roughly 70% US.
>
> You sell submission management software to exactly these people.
>
> One sponsor slot per issue, $600/week for four issues. You get 40 words and a link at
> the top of the brief. I'll write it in the brief's voice if you'd like — sponsors that
> read like the newsletter perform considerably better than ad copy.
>
> Happy to send the last two weeks of issues so you can see what you'd be next to.
>
> — [name]

Why it works: measured numbers with a stated measurement window rather than vague
claims, audience composition rather than raw size, an explicit statement of why *this*
sponsor fits, a price stated immediately, and an offer to see the product first. Note
"measured over the last 30 days" — that phrasing is a G3 requirement, not politeness.
If you have not measured it, do not send it.
