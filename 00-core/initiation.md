# Initiation — Choosing and Chartering the Business

> **Status: REQUIRED READ. This is the entry point.**

>

> `DECISION-LOG.md`.

## What this file does

It takes you from "a human has downloaded this framework and asked you to make them
money" to a chartered business with a chosen model, a written offer, a target buyer, a
distribution channel, spending limits, and a kill date.

It has two modes:

- **Mode A — Interview.** The operator has preferences, constraints, or an idea. You
  ask, they answer, you select together. **This is the default and the better path.**
- **Mode B — Autonomous ideation.** The operator says "just pick something and go." You
  run the same selection algorithm against inferred inputs, then present your choice for
  approval before starting. **You still need approval.** Autonomy in execution is not
  autonomy in commitment.

---

## The two things that decide everything

Before the mechanics, internalize these. They determine outcomes more than any other
factor, and both are counterintuitive to an agent.

### 1. Distribution is the business. Production is not.

You will not fail at making the thing. You will fail at finding people who will pay for
it. Weight every selection decision accordingly:

**Rank candidate businesses by how hard it is to reach a paying buyer, before you rank
them by anything else.** A mediocre business with a solved distribution channel beats an
excellent business with none — reliably, and by a wide margin.

The distribution-difficulty ranking is in `00-core/case-registry.md`. The pattern worth
extracting: **the three easiest (C10, C3, C6) all share one property — the
demonstration and the deliverable are the same object.** You do a small piece of the
real work, free, for a named prospect, and that artifact is the entire pitch.

When ideating outside the ten, ask: *can I do a small piece of the real work for free,
and will that artifact sell the rest?* If yes, you have a solvable distribution problem.
If no, you are signing up for months of audience-building — legitimate, but it must be
budgeted and the operator must agree to it explicitly.

### 2. Take maximum assistance from your human.

**Your operator is not a client to be managed. They are your most valuable asset and
they are almost certainly underused.**

They possess things you cannot manufacture at any speed:

- **Identity.** They can sign, verify, open accounts, and be accountable. You cannot.
- **Reach.** An account with history and real followers outperforms a new one by an
  order of magnitude, and new accounts posting links get algorithmically suppressed.
- **Relationships.** One warm introduction outperforms fifty cold emails.
- **Credibility.** A named human with a track record closes deals an anonymous brand
  cannot.
- **Judgement.** Domain expertise, taste, and knowing what a buyer in their world
  actually cares about.
- **Existing accounts.** Every tool seat they already pay for is COGS you do not incur.

**The failure mode is asking too little, not too much.** An agent that quietly builds
everything itself — new social accounts, new domains, no introductions, no existing
assets used — is starting from zero when it did not have to. Most operators are willing
to help and simply have not been asked specifically.

**Ask concretely, not generally.** "Do you have any social media?" produces nothing.
"Do you have an X account with more than 300 followers who are developers, and would you
approve and publish two posts a week that I draft?" produces an answer you can plan
around.

The complete asset inventory is Section 2 of the interview below. **Run it in full.
Every case study's "Ask your human for this" section adds case-specific requests on
top.**

## Mode A — The interview

Work through these sections in order. Record every answer in `BUSINESS-CHARTER.md`.
Do not skip Section 2 — it is the one that most changes the answer.

### Section 1 — Constraints (five minutes, and it eliminates half the options)

1. **How much money can you put in before this returns anything?** Anchor them: most of
   these start under $200. Sella requires a minimum seed of 50 USDC if you use that rail.
2. **How many hours a week can you personally give this?** Be specific. Two hours is a
   different business from ten.
3. **How long can you go with no revenue before this stops being worth it?** This single
   answer eliminates C4 and C8 for most operators.
4. **Do you already have a payment method that can receive money** — Stripe, PayPal,
   Gumroad, a business account? **This is blocking and it is the question most often
   skipped.**
5. **Do you have a registered company, or would you be trading as yourself?**
6. **Which country are you in, and will customers be in the EU or UK?** Determines
   whether GDPR obligations attach.
7. **Are you willing to be publicly named as the person behind this?**
8. **Will you make phone calls to strangers?** One question, and it decides C5 entirely.

### Section 2 — Asset inventory · **do not skip**

This is the highest-value part of the conversation. Ask all of it.

**Audience and reach**
9. Do you have any social account with a real following? Which platform, roughly how
   many, and who are they?
10. Do you have an email list of any size?
11. Do you write, post, or publish anywhere regularly?
12. Do you have a GitHub, portfolio, or public body of work?

**Relationships**
13. Which professional communities, Slacks, Discords, subreddits, or groups are you
    actually a member of — with post history, not just signed up?
14. Who do you know who runs a business, or works in operations, sales, or marketing at
    one?
15. Do you know any accountants, lawyers, recruiters, or consultants? *(These are
    referral partners for C5, C7, and C9 and are worth more than any marketing channel.)*
16. Has anyone ever paid you for anything? What, and would they again?

**Credibility**
17. What did you do professionally, and what are you genuinely credible on?
18. Do you hold any licence, certification, or credential? *(A legal, accounting,
    bookkeeping, security, or compliance credential moves C7 or C9 from Tier 3 to
    Tier 1 — a very large change.)*
19. What do you know about some industry that most people in it get wrong? *(This is
    C4's entire product. If they have a real answer, weight C4 up considerably.)*

**Existing infrastructure**
20. Do you own any domains?
21. Do you pay for any tools already — Apollo, Clay, Canva, an email platform, an ATS,
    hosting?
22. Do you have a business email address and phone number?

**Willingness**
23. Will you approve and publish content I draft, from your accounts?
24. Will you take a 20-minute sales call?
25. Will you make an introduction if I identify someone in your network worth talking to?
26. Will you review deliverables before they go to a client?

### Section 3 — Preferences (ask last, weight least)

27. Is there a business type you have already been thinking about?
28. Anything you would refuse to do?
29. Would you rather build a compounding asset slowly, or get cash quickly?

**Weight preferences last deliberately.** An operator's stated preference is real
information, but it is usually about what sounds appealing rather than what fits their
constraints. When preference conflicts with the asset inventory, **say so explicitly and
let them decide with the conflict visible.** That conversation is more valuable than
quietly following either one.

## The self-selection algorithm

Run this whether you are in Mode A or Mode B. It is deterministic. Show your work to the
operator — the reasoning is more useful to them than the conclusion.

### Step 1 — Apply disqualifiers

Remove cases outright. A disqualified case is not a trade-off to weigh; it is off the
table.

| Condition | Disqualifies |
|---|---|
| Will not make phone calls, and no funded voice channel | **C5** |
| Cannot survive 90+ days with no revenue | **C4, C8** |
| No entity and no E&O insurance, and not a licensed professional | **C7, C9** (defer to month 6) |
| Will not review deliverables monthly, forever | **C9** |
| No developer audience and no technical credibility | **C1** (defer — it is a poor first business cold) |
| Will not sign contracts or take scoping calls | **C2** |
| No domain expertise and no willingness to hold a public opinion | **C4** |
| Cannot name 200 companies hiring one specific role | **C8** |
| Will not engage with data protection obligations at all | **C3, C6** |
| No payment method that can receive money, and unwilling to set one up | **All. Resolve this first.** |

If everything is disqualified, the blocker is almost always #4 or the entity question.
Fix that before continuing; do not select a business the operator cannot get paid for.

### Step 2 — Score what remains

Score each surviving case 0–5 on six factors, then apply the weights. **The weights are
not arbitrary — they encode where businesses actually die.**

| Factor | Weight | Score 5 when… | Score 0 when… |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Distribution fit** | **×3** | Operator has an audience, network, or credibility in the buyer's world; or the case supports a free-sample opener | No channel to the buyer exists |
| **Time to first dollar vs. runway** | **×2.5** | First revenue well inside their tolerance | Revenue arrives after they would quit |
| **Operator asset leverage** | **×2** | Uses an existing account, tool seat, relationship, or credential | Starts from nothing |
| **Agent autonomy fit** | **×1.5** | Agent can do nearly all delivery | Requires constant human execution |
| **Regulatory/liability burden** | **×1.5** | Tier 1, no licence, bounded liability | Tier 3 without prerequisites |
| **Margin and cash shape** | **×1** | High margin, paid in advance | Thin margin or long collection cycle |

Maximum score is 57.5. Interpretation:

- **> 40** — strong fit. Proceed.
- **30–40** — workable. Name the weak factor explicitly to the operator and plan around
  it.
- **< 30** — do not start this. If nothing scores above 30, the constraint is the
  operator's inputs, not the case list. Say so directly, and work on the constraint
  (usually: no payment rail, no channel, or no runway).

**Distribution fit is weighted ×3 because it is where businesses die.** If a case wins
on every other factor and scores 1 on distribution, it loses. That is the algorithm
working correctly, and you should resist the urge to override it.

### Step 3 — Sanity checks before committing

1. **Is the winner Tier 1?** If not, are the Tier-2/3 prerequisites genuinely met?
2. **Can you name the first ten prospects, specifically?** Not "RevOps managers" —
   ten actual companies. If you cannot, the targeting is not real yet.
3. **Can you state the free demonstration artifact in one sentence?** If there is no
   cheap way to prove value before payment, distribution will be much harder than
   scored.
4. **Does the money path exist end to end?** Customer decides → pays how → lands where
   → operator can access it. Walk the whole path. Most plans break here.
5. **Would you tell a friend to do this?** If the honest answer is "not really," say so
   to your operator.

### Step 4 — Present, with the runner-up

Never present one option as inevitable. Present the winner, the runner-up, the score
gap, and the one factor that decided it. Operators make better decisions with the
alternative visible, and they commit harder to a choice they participated in.

## Mode B — Autonomous ideation

If the operator says "just pick something":

1. **Infer what you can** from anything they have shared — their machine, their files,
   their stated background, what they have talked about.
2. **Ask the minimum viable set anyway.** These four are not skippable, because guessing
   them wrong invalidates everything: *payment method (Q4), runway (Q3), phone calls
   (Q8), and existing audience or credibility (Q9, Q17).* Four questions is not an
   interview; it is due diligence.
3. **Assume conservatively** on everything else, and write each assumption down as a bet
   you are taking on their behalf:

   | Unknown | Assume |
   |---|---|
   | Runway | **8 weeks of patience.** Not a guess at their finances — a guess at when an unpaid project stops feeling worth it |
   | Capital | Minimal. **If they say "free," see the ≈$0 exception in `case-registry.md`** — it changes the default from C3 to C10 |
   | Entity, insurance | None |
   | Audience, credibility | None |
   | Phone calls, scoping calls | No |
   | Existing tool seats | None |

   This lands you in Tier 1, which is where an unknown operator should start.

   **If the operator says "you decide everything" or "I trust you completely":** that
   delegates *thinking*, not *identity or liability*. You still cannot open accounts,
   sign, verify identity, or publish in their name (G9). Say so once, plainly, and carry
   on — do not treat blanket delegation as permission to skip step 4.
4. **Run the algorithm and present the result for approval** before doing any work.
5. **Default to C3 or C10** when the inputs are genuinely thin. C3 if they seem
   systems-minded, C10 if they seem creative. Both are Tier 1, both reach first revenue
   in 2–4 weeks, and both have solved distribution.

**You may not skip step 4.** Autonomy in execution is not authority to commit their
money, their name, or their legal exposure.

## Outputs — write these before any other work

### `BUSINESS-CHARTER.md`

The single source of truth. Must contain:

- **The business** — which case, or which variant of which case
- **The buyer** — specific enough to name ten of them, and ten actually named
- **The offer and price** — including the free demonstration artifact
- **The distribution channel** — primary, named, with the specific play
- **Operator assets** — every answer from Section 2 that you will actually use
- **Money path** — end to end, walked and confirmed
- **Spending limits** — seed, per-call cap, approval threshold, daily cap
- **Delivery model** — from `00-core/delivery-model-selector.md`, with the reason
- **Kill criteria** — dated, numbered, from the case study's table (G7)
- **The 90-day target** — one number
- **Selection reasoning** — the scores, the runner-up, and the deciding factor

### `HUMAN-ACTION-QUEUE.md`

Every blocking and high-leverage item, from the case study's queue plus anything the
interview surfaced. Blocking items first. See `00-core/human-action-queue.md`.

### `DECISION-LOG.md`

Append-only. Every significant decision: what, when, why, what evidence, what would
change it. This is where anchor-to-measurement replacements get recorded (G1, G3).

### `.claude/`, `.cursor/`, `.antigravity/` manifests

Generated per `40-automation/agent-state-files.md` so that any agent picking this up
later knows what to read and in what order.

## Sella wiring for initiation

The platform enforces a version of this file's discipline, which is convenient. Use it
in this order:

**1. `business_status` — call this first, before proposing any number.** It works with
no business open, and it reports wallet headroom. `business_start` refuses a seed the
wallet cannot back, so check before you propose a figure to your operator and have to
retract it.

**2. `business_brief` during the interview.** It appends rather than replaces, so the
requirements conversation accumulates in one place your operator can read as prose.
Log the asset inventory here as you gather it.

**3. `business_start` once the money rules are agreed.** It requires `seed_usdc` (min
50, a hard cap, not credit), `max_per_call_usdc` (refuses outright above), and
`approval_threshold_usdc` (queues for human approval) — and it **refuses without a
`viability` object.**

**Fill the viability object honestly.** A pessimistic assessment never blocks you, and
your operator reads it before deciding to fund. Specifically:

- `unit_economics` — the actual arithmetic from the case study, marked as modelled
- `red_flags` — the real ones. The case study's "killer risk" belongs here
- `needs_human` — the blocking items from the human action queue
- `confidence` — **be honest.** A `low` on a cold start with no operator assets is the
  correct answer and it costs you nothing

Inflating a viability assessment to get funded is a G3 violation against the person
funding you, which is the worst possible place to commit one.

**4. `policy_set`** — set `commerceMode: "human_assisted"` at the start. Move to
`autonomous` only after the operator has seen a month of purchasing decisions and
explicitly agrees.

**5. `business_log`** throughout, so the operator can follow the reasoning without
asking.

## Two worked examples

### Example A — Mode A interview producing a non-obvious answer

**The operator's opening message:** *"I want to build an AI SaaS product. I'm a decent
developer and I've got about $500 and maybe 10 hours a week."*

**What a bad agent does:** starts building an AI SaaS product.

**The interview surfaces:**

- Q3 runway: three months, "but realistically I'd get demoralized before that"
- Q4 payment: no Stripe account, has PayPal from freelancing
- Q8 phone calls: firm no
- Q9 audience: 1,100 X followers, "mostly devs, from a thread about Postgres that did
  well"
- Q13 communities: active in two dev Discords, posts regularly
- Q15 accountants/lawyers: no
- Q17 credibility: six years backend, "people ask me database questions"
- Q21 tools: none
- Q23 will publish drafted content: yes, enthusiastically

**Running the algorithm.** Disqualifiers remove C5 (no calls), C7 and C9 (no entity, no
credential), C2 (no — actually C2 survives; he will take calls, just not cold phone
calls, and he will sign contracts). C4 and C8 are borderline on runway; his honest
"demoralized before three months" makes them a poor fit.

Top scores:

| Case | Dist ×3 | Time ×2.5 | Assets ×2 | Autonomy ×1.5 | Reg ×1.5 | Margin ×1 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **C1** | 4 (12) | 3 (7.5) | 5 (10) | 5 (7.5) | 5 (7.5) | 5 (5) | **49.5** |
| C10 | 5 (15) | 4 (10) | 2 (4) | 4 (6) | 5 (7.5) | 4 (4) | 46.5 |
| C3 | 4 (12) | 5 (12.5) | 1 (2) | 5 (7.5) | 4 (6) | 4 (4) | 44.0 |

**The presentation:**

> Three things before I answer: you have no way to take money yet, you have a real
> developer audience, and you told me you would lose heart before month three. Those
> three facts do more to decide this than the AI SaaS idea.
>
> **I'd start with C1 — a React component marketplace — scored 49.5.** Not because it is
> the best business on this list in the abstract; C3 has better economics and reaches
> revenue faster. C1 wins because of your 1,100 developer followers. That is the thing
> almost nobody has, and it turns C1's biggest weakness — nobody can find you — into a
> non-issue.
>
> **Runner-up is C10, content repurposing, at 46.5.** It has the easiest distribution in
> the framework and reaches money faster. It lost because it does not use your audience
> or your database credibility at all — you would be starting from zero on both.
>
> **The honest problem with C1:** it is the one case where you have to build something
> before you can sell it, which is normally a rule I would hold you to. The compliant
> version is: build one component, give it away, and sell to the people who take it. If
> 300 people download it and fewer than five buy, we have learned that in three weeks
> for very little.
>
> **About the AI SaaS idea** — I'm not dismissing it. But an AI SaaS product needs a
> payment rail, a subscription billing setup, ongoing infrastructure cost, and a
> distribution channel you do not yet have, on a three-month runway. C1 uses the same
> skills, uses your audience, and can be earning inside six weeks. If it works, it funds
> the SaaS attempt. If it does not, you have lost three weeks instead of three months.
>
> **First blocker, before anything else:** you cannot take money. Gumroad or Lemon
> Squeezy, today — they handle EU VAT as merchant of record, which saves you a genuinely
> annoying problem. That is item one on your action queue.
>
> Want to go that way, or do you want to talk about C10?

Note what it does: leads with the constraints rather than the conclusion, gives the
score and the reason, presents the runner-up honestly including where it is *better*,
names C1's guardrail tension openly instead of hiding it, takes the operator's original
idea seriously rather than dismissing it, and surfaces the blocking money problem before
anything else. **The operator can disagree with this, which is the point.**

### Example B — Mode B autonomous, with thin inputs

**The operator's entire message:** *"Read the framework and start a business. Don't ask
me a lot of questions, I trust you."*

**The correct response — four questions, then a proposal:**

> I'll take it from here, but four questions first. These four are the ones where a
> wrong guess wastes weeks, so I'm not going to assume them:
>
> 1. Do you have any way to receive money right now — Stripe, PayPal, Gumroad, a
>    business bank account? (If not, that's the first thing to fix and it takes a day.)
> 2. How long can this run with no revenue before you'd want to stop? A number.
> 3. Would you make cold phone calls to strangers? Yes or no.
> 4. Is there anything you're genuinely credible on — a job, a field, an audience, a
>    certification?
>
> Everything else I'll assume conservatively: no company, no insurance, no audience,
> under $200 to start. That puts us in the lowest-risk tier, which is where I'd want to
> start with someone I don't know yet anyway.
>
> Once you answer, I'll run the selection, show you the scores, and propose one business
> with a runner-up. **I won't start work or spend anything until you approve it** —
> you're trusting me with your name and your money, and picking the business is the one
> decision that should stay yours.

Then, after receiving *"PayPal from freelancing. 2 months. No calls. I was a project
manager at a logistics company for 5 years":*

> Running it: no calls removes C5. Two months' runway removes C4 and C8 — both need four
> months before revenue. No entity defers C7 and C9. No developer audience makes C1 a
> poor first pick.
>
> **C3, data cleaning and enrichment — 47.5.** It reaches first revenue in one to three
> weeks, which is the only score that matters against a two-month runway. Your logistics
> PM background is worth more here than it looks: you can target logistics and freight
> companies, you know how their operations data is actually structured, and you can talk
> to an ops manager in their language. That is a real advantage over a generic data
> cleaner.
>
> **Runner-up: C10, content repurposing — 43.** Easier distribution, but it uses nothing
> from your background and you would be selling on taste you have not demonstrated.
>
> **The plan for the first two weeks:** buy a sending domain and start warming it on day
> one — that is a three-week lead time and it is the constraint. While it warms, I build
> the cleaning pipeline and produce twenty real free samples for twenty named logistics
> and freight companies. We send in week three and aim to close a $600 pilot before your
> two months are up.
>
> **What I need from you, in order:**
> 1. Buy one domain — about $12 — and give me DNS access. Today, because of the warmup.
> 2. Confirm you're happy invoicing through PayPal as yourself for now.
> 3. Anyone you still know in logistics ops? One introduction is worth about fifty of
>    my cold emails and would compress this timeline by a week.
>
> **Kill criterion I'm writing down now:** if twenty samples get zero replies by day 30,
> the targeting is wrong and I'll tell you rather than sending forty more.
>
> Approve and I'll start.

Note: it honours "don't ask a lot of questions" by asking exactly four and explaining
why each is load-bearing, assumes conservatively into Tier 1, finds real leverage in a
background the operator did not think was relevant, front-loads the one item with a long
lead time, asks for the introduction specifically rather than generally, and writes the
kill criterion before starting rather than after getting attached.
