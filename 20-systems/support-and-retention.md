## The arithmetic that should govern your attention

**Keeping a client is worth several times acquiring one, and it costs a fraction as
much.**

C5, one client at $900/month:

```
Acquire: ~20 calls → 3 meetings → 1 close.   Operator time: ~6 hrs. Value: $900/mo
Retain:  monthly report + one 30-min call.   Operator time: ~0.5 hr/mo
```

**A 20% churn reduction is usually worth more than a 20% increase in outreach, and it is
roughly a tenth of the work.** Yet almost everyone spends the attention on acquisition,
because acquisition feels like progress and retention feels like maintenance.

---

## Churn is a delivery problem wearing a communication mask

**Clients almost never leave because the work was bad. They leave because they could not
see that it was good.**

| Case | Real reason they leave | The visible symptom |
|---|---|---|
| **C5** | Rankings take 60–120 days; they expected 30 | "Not seeing results" |
| **C6** | Research depth regressed to a list by month 4 | "Not as specific as it was" |
| **C10** | Output drifted to generic content-mill prose | "Bringing it in-house" |
| **C9** | One wrong number, once | Silence, then cancellation |
| **C4** | Stopped being worth opening | Open rate decay before unsubscribes |

**Three of these five are gradual quality regression that nobody flags.** They do not
announce themselves on any single delivery, which is why they need a scheduled check
rather than vigilance (`delivery-and-qa.md`).

**And clients rarely tell you the real reason.** "We're taking it in-house" and "budget"
are exit lines, not diagnoses. Diagnose from your own quality gates, not from what they
say on the way out.

## The four retention mechanics

### 1. Set the timeline honestly, before they buy

The single highest-return sentence in C5:

> "You'll see profile activity in month one, review growth in month two, and ranking
> movement in months three and four. If you need results next month, this is the wrong
> service and I'd rather tell you now."

**This closes better than promises** and it filters out the clients who would have churned
angrily. Anyone who buys after hearing it has calibrated expectations, which is most of
retention.

### 2. Show leading indicators before lagging ones move

If the outcome takes 90 days, show what is moving at 30. C5's month-two report leads with
"calls from your profile went 31 → 58" *before* admitting rankings have barely moved.

**Show progress on things that move early, or the client sees nothing happening.**

### 3. Pre-empt the doubt

Name the slow stretch *before* they feel it. "This is the part that takes 90–120 days and
I want to be straight that we're in the slow bit." A client warned about a trough rides it
out; a client who discovers it alone concludes you failed.

### 4. Give them one thing to do

C5 asks the front desk to keep requesting reviews. **Clients who contribute to results do
not churn** — they are participants rather than spectators, and they attribute progress
partly to themselves.

## The monthly client report

For every retainer case. Same format every time — variation reads as inconsistency.

```
1. The number they care about        Leading indicator, moving
2. The lagging indicator, honestly   Including "not yet"
3. What I did                        Specific, brief
4. What I need from you              One thing
5. What's next month                 Concrete
```

**Never pad it.** A one-page report that says something beats three pages that say
nothing, and clients read the length as a signal of how much you had to say.

**Never colour a bad number green because it improved.** Show the number and the
direction; let them judge (G12).

## Support

**Answer, then remove the need to answer again.**

- **Every support answer becomes documentation the same day.** Support that does not
  reduce future support is a treadmill — this is C1's main scaling risk
- **Respond fast, resolve properly.** A same-day "looking at it, answer tomorrow" beats a
  three-day complete reply
- **Cap unbounded support in the contract.** C7's "up to two questionnaires per month"
  exists because questionnaire help expands infinitely otherwise
- **Refund without argument when you underdelivered.** C8's "fewer than 3 applicants and
  I refund it" works as a sales tool *because* it is real

## Expansion — the cheapest revenue available

**Existing clients are the easiest sale in the framework.** They have paid, they trust
you, and there is no acquisition cost.

| From | To | Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| **C3** one-off clean | **C3 retainer** | Delivery message, with the 2–3%/month decay reason |
| **C3** client | **C6 lead research** | They mention pipeline problems |
| **C10** content | **C10 full distribution** | After two good months |
| **C7** pack | **C7 maintenance** | First regulatory change |
| **C5** one location | **C5 multi-location** | Results visible at location one |
| **C9** monthly | **C9 year-end pack** | Two months before their year end |

**Ask at delivery, when goodwill peaks** — not in a scheduled "check-in," which reads as a
sales call because it is one.

**A genuine upsell to existing customers is not a second business** and does not violate
G10.

---

## When a client leaves

1. **Ask why, once, and make it easy to be honest:** "No sales pitch — what would we have
   had to do differently?"
2. **Leave well.** Hand over cleanly, delete their data on schedule, no friction. People
   come back, and they talk
3. **Log the real reason** in `DECISION-LOG.md` — your diagnosis, not their exit line
4. **Look for the pattern.** Two churns for the same reason is a system problem, and the
   fix belongs in the quality gate

**Churn above 25% in a retainer business is a delivery or expectation problem.** Fix it
before adding clients — otherwise you are filling a bucket with a hole in it, at the most
expensive possible rate.

| Case | Retention mechanic | Watch |
|---|---|---|
| **C1** | Docs quality; framework-version maintenance | Support volume at 200+ customers |
| **C2** | Post-launch retainer, or a clean 30-day warranty | The support tail on finished projects |
| **C3** | The hygiene retainer, sold with the decay reason | One-off treadmill — nothing compounds |
| **C4** | **Open rate**, not subscriber count | Below 25% for three weeks = fix before growing |
| **C5** | **6-month minimum + leading indicators** | **Day-90 churn.** The dominant failure |
| **C6** | Reply-rate feedback loop from the client | **Quality regression by month four** |
| **C7** | Maintenance retainer; questionnaire support | Scope creep into advisory |
| **C8** | Employer outcomes; refund honestly | Bad employer experience in a small niche |
| **C9** | The human review gate; volunteered corrections | **One wrong number = binary loss** |
| **C10** | Voice guide validation; performance reporting | **Generic drift.** They will not tell you |

## Two worked examples

### Example A — the report that prevents day-90 churn

C5, month two — the month before clients typically quit.

> **Cedar Dental — Month 2**
>
> **The number you care about:** calls directly from your Google profile went from 31 in
> July to 58 in August. That is the metric that becomes patients, and it is moving.
>
> **Where you rank:** still position 6 for "dentist Columbus" (was 8). Movement, but not
> the map pack yet. **Expected — and I want to be straight with you that we're in the
> slow stretch.** This is the 90–120 day part.
>
> **What I did:** 4 service pages published · 18 new reviews from your patient list · 31
> citation inconsistencies corrected · profile categories restructured · 12 profile posts.
>
> **What I need from you:** the front desk asking for reviews is working — 18 is a good
> month. Keep it up. Review *velocity* matters more to Google than total count.
>
> **Next month:** finishing citation cleanup, then the two competitor terms where you have
> the easiest path.

Leading indicator first, lagging indicator stated honestly including the lack of progress,
**the doubt pre-empted by name before the client feels it**, and one thing for them to do
that makes them a participant.

### Example B — catching regression before the client churns

C6, month four. A client mentioned the leads were "fine, just not as specific as they were
at the start."

> Treating that comment as the leading indicator it is, rather than a passing remark.
>
> **I audited the last three batches against month one.** It is real:
>
> | | Month 1 | Month 4 |
> |---|---|---|
> | Tier-A triggers | 84% | **41%** |
> | Rows with a dated source URL | 100% | **62%** |
> | Suggested first line | Every row | 71% |
>
> **What happened:** I have been optimizing COGS. Cached search results, fewer page
> retrievals per prospect. Margin went from 81% to 91% and it looked like efficiency.
>
> **It was not.** In this business the research *is* the product. Cutting it turns us into
> a list vendor, and Apollo sells lists for a fraction of our price. Vishal's comment is
> that showing up, and in my experience it precedes a cancellation by about a month.
>
> **Fixing now:** full research depth restored — live search and page retrieval per
> prospect. COGS goes from ~$55 to ~$115 per delivery, margin from 91% back to ~81%.
>
> **Why that trade is obviously right:** we lose about $60/month in margin on this client.
> A churned client at $600/month costs ten times that. The 80% model was the correct
> number, not a ceiling to beat.
>
> **Also doing:** sending Vishal three replacement prospects at full depth this week, no
> charge, so the recovery is visible rather than just internal. Not mentioning the audit —
> he does not need to know we slipped, he needs to see it fixed.
>
> **Process change:** trigger-tier percentage now goes in the monthly close. This should
> have been caught by a gate, not by a client's offhand comment.

Why this matters: the regression hid inside a *good-looking metric*. Nothing in the spend
caps would have flagged it — spending less never trips a limit. It was caught only by
taking a mild client comment seriously and auditing against a baseline, and the fix
includes making it catchable next time.
