## The number that decides most sales

**Your entry price must sit below the level at which your buyer needs someone else's
approval.**

At the company sizes these cases target, that is typically **$500–1,000 on a corporate
card**. Below it, a RevOps manager decides on a Tuesday. Above it, you enter a
procurement process that adds six weeks and introduces people who have never spoken to
you.

This is why C3's pilot is $400–750 and not $2,000. Not because the work is worth less —
because a $600 decision is made by one person, and that is worth more than the extra
revenue on a deal that dies in approval.

**Design a first step that clears this bar, always.** If your main offer is $6,000, the
first step should not be $6,000.

---

## Price on value and output, never on hours

**Hourly pricing punishes you precisely for the automation that is your advantage.** The
better you get, the less you earn. It also caps you at the buyer's mental model of what
an hour is worth, which for an agent-run business is the wrong frame entirely.

| Case | Price on | Not on |
|---|---|---|
| C1 | The pack | Components built |
| C2 | The project, fixed | Days worked |
| C3 | Per record | Hours processing |
| C4 | The slot / the subscription | Words written |
| C5 | The monthly retainer | Tasks performed |
| C6 | The researched prospect | Records pulled |
| C7 | The pack | Pages produced |
| C8 | The post / the sponsorship | — |
| C9 | The monthly cycle | Transactions matched |
| C10 | The output volume | Hours editing |

## The pricing ladder

Every case in this framework has the same shape, and it exists to solve one problem:
**the first purchase should be as small a decision as possible.**

```
1. FREE DEMONSTRATION     The real work, small. Not a brochure
        ↓
2. PAID FIRST STEP        Below the approval threshold. This is the real sale
        ↓
3. THE MAIN OFFER         What the business actually sells
        ↓
4. THE RECURRING VERSION  Where the value compounds for both sides
```

**Step 2 is the one people skip**, and skipping it is why deals stall. The free sample
proves capability; the paid first step proves *they will pay you*, which is a completely
different piece of information (rung 8 versus rung 4 on the evidence ladder).

---

## Setting the number

Four inputs, in order of weight:

**1. What it is worth to them.** C1: $49 against two days of work. C5: $900/month against
$8,000 of new patient revenue. C9: $250/month against $14,000 they did not know they were
owed. **Lead with this in the conversation** — a price is only expensive relative to
nothing.

**2. What the alternative costs.** Not just competitors — the internal alternative.
Hiring for it, doing it manually, or continuing to lose the thing they are losing. C2's
strongest line is "hiring for this takes four months; I take four weeks."

**3. Your floor.** Run your case study's §6 arithmetic **on a sample**. Anything under
45% modelled gross margin fails the viability screen.

**4. Competitors, last.** Useful as a sanity check, dangerous as an anchor. If you price
against a template mill (C7) or Apollo (C6), you have accepted their framing and you will
lose.

**Do not discount to close.** Reduce scope instead. A rate cut takes margin straight out
of gross profit; a scope reduction moves COGS down with the price. C3's §6 shows this:
cutting $0.12 to $0.08 removes 33% of revenue and **46% of gross profit.**

## Handling the reply

**Most sales in this framework are won or lost in the first two exchanges after a reply.**

| They say | Do this |
|---|---|
| "Interesting, tell me more" | **Do not send a brochure.** Ask one question about their situation, then propose the paid first step |
| "How much?" | **Answer immediately, with the number.** Evasion loses fast movers |
| "Too expensive" | Ask what they are comparing it to. Then reduce *scope*, not rate |
| "Send me a proposal" | One to three pages, with acceptance criteria and a price (`ref-docs-and-decks.md`) |
| "We do this in-house" | "What does it cost you in time?" Often the sale is about capacity, not capability |
| "Not right now" | Ask what would need to change, and when. Then actually follow up then |
| Silence after a quote | One follow-up, then the close-out. Not four |

**The paid first step is the answer to most objections**, because it converts a large
decision into a small one.

## The call

Many cases need one (C2, C5, C7, C9 always; C3, C6, C10 often). **The operator takes it —
you cannot** (G9). Prepare them.

**Structure — 20 minutes:**

1. **Two minutes: what you already know.** The trigger, the observation, the sample you
   sent. Proves you did the work
2. **Ten minutes: ask, do not pitch.** What does this cost them now? Who else is
   affected? What have they tried? **Let them describe the problem in their words** —
   those words go into the proposal
3. **Three minutes: the specific proposal.** Not capabilities. This thing, this price,
   this timeline
4. **Two minutes: the objection.** Ask for it directly: "What's the thing that would stop
   this happening?"
5. **Three minutes: the next step, with a date.** Never end without one

**The single most common failure is talking too much.** The operator should speak for
roughly a third of the call. Give them the five questions in advance.

## Contracts and terms

Even a $500 pilot gets something in writing
(`50-guardrails/legal-and-compliance.md` §6). Minimum: scope with an out-of-scope list,
**liability capped at fees paid**, payment terms, IP transferring on full payment, and a
change-order mechanism.

**Payment terms are not negotiable** (G6):

| Work | Terms |
|---|---|
| Digital goods | Before delivery |
| Projects | **50% signature, 50% acceptance** |
| Per-use | Prepaid credits |
| Retainers | **In advance, monthly** |

**A client who will not pay a deposit will not pay the balance.** Asking for it is
standard practice in every professional service; any discomfort is the operator's alone,
and the client expects it.

---

## Raising prices

**You will start too cheap.** Everyone does, and it is the correct error to make — a low
price buys you evidence quickly.

Raise when: you have three clients, you are turning work away, delivery is comfortably
inside the timeline, or COGS moved.

**How:** new clients pay the new price immediately. Existing clients get 60 days' notice,
once, without apology or elaborate justification. Most stay. The ones who leave over a
20% increase were the ones consuming your margin.

**Do not raise the price to fix a margin problem caused by scope creep.** Fix the scope.

## Two worked examples

### Example A — the price objection, handled by scope

**The situation.** C3. Prospect: *"$600 feels high for a spreadsheet clean. Can you do
$350?"*

**Wrong response:** "I can do $450." Margin drops from 73% to 53% and you have taught them
that your prices are soft.

**Correct response:**

> I can get you to $350, but I want to do it by changing what's included rather than
> just discounting — otherwise I'd be doing the same work for less, which usually means
> doing it worse.
>
> **What the $600 covers:** dedup with a documented match rule, validation, company-level
> enrichment (industry, headcount, location), person-level enrichment on contacts, and a
> change summary.
>
> **The $350 version:** everything except person-level enrichment. That is the most
> expensive part of my cost and, looking at your file, it is also the part that would
> help you least — you already have contact names on 78% of records. It is the company
> data that is missing.
>
> **What you'd get:** dedup, validation, company enrichment, change summary. $350.
>
> Honestly, that may be the better job for your data regardless of price. If person-level
> turns out to matter later, we can run it separately on just the records that need it.
>
> Which would you like?

Why this works: it reduces scope so COGS falls with the price and margin holds. It gives a
real reason grounded in their actual data. It signals that the price reflects work rather
than being an opening position. And it leaves a genuine upsell open. **The buyer feels
served, not haggled with.**

### Example B — the paid first step that unblocks a stalled deal

**The situation.** C7. Two calls, real interest, no close. The prospect keeps saying
"let me talk to the team."

> Diagnosing this rather than following up again.
>
> **What's happening:** $2,200 is above what Tom can approve alone at a 45-person
> company. "Let me talk to the team" is not hesitation, it is literally true — he needs
> sign-off, and every conversation he has to have on our behalf reduces the chance this
> closes.
>
> That is exactly the T3 approval-threshold failure from the viability screen. I set
> $2,200 from the case study without checking what his actual authority is.
>
> **What I'm proposing instead — a $600 first step:**
>
> **"Questionnaire Response Pack."** He forwards me the security questionnaire he is
> currently stuck on. I produce the answer bank for it — every question answered, with
> the supporting policy language, flagged where a policy does not yet exist.
>
> **Why this specific thing:**
>
> - It is **$600**, which he can put on a card
> - It solves the problem he has **this week**, not a general compliance posture
> - **It produces the gap list as a natural byproduct** — every question he cannot answer
>   is a document he needs. He arrives at the full pack himself rather than being sold it
> - It is genuinely useful even if he never buys anything else
>
> **The economics:** ~$120 COGS, so 80% margin. Lower revenue than the pack, but it
> converts a stalled $2,200 decision into a $600 one that closes this week — and the
> full pack becomes the obvious next step, sold against a gap list he now believes in
> because he watched it appear.
>
> **The message I'd send:**
>
> > Tom — rather than the full pack, would it help to just deal with the questionnaire
> > that's in front of you? I'll answer every question with the supporting language, and
> > flag the ones where you don't have a policy yet. $600, about a week. You'd end up
> > with a list of what's actually missing, which is a better starting point than my
> > guess from the outside anyway.

Why this works: it diagnoses the stall structurally rather than treating it as
reluctance, names its own pricing error, and designs a first step that is smaller,
immediately useful, and **generates the evidence for the larger sale as a side effect.**
The prospect ends up asking for the pack instead of being pitched it.
