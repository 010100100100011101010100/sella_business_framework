Vectors, Calculus & Advanced 2D Kinematics

[MENTOR STRATEGY NOTE] Welcome to Mechanics

Listen up: Mechanics isn't a collection of 50 disconnected equations to memorize the night before the exam. It's a game of reference frames, constraint geometry, and calculus. In JEE Advanced, Kinematics rarely appears as a standalone trivial question—it shows up as the first layer inside rotational dynamics, electromagnetic Lorentz trajectories, and variable mass systems.

The Golden Rule: The moment you see variable acceleration $a(t), a(v),$ or $a(x)$, throw your high-school kinematic equations ($v = u + at, s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2$) straight out the window. If you use them when acceleration changes, you are handing the examiner an easy -1.

1.1 First-Principles Foundations: The Calculus of Motion

Let's strip kinematics down to first principles. Where an object is located relative to an origin $O$ is described by its position vector:

$$\mathbf{r}(t) = x(t)\hat{\mathbf{i}} + y(t)\hat{\mathbf{j}} + z(t)\hat{\mathbf{k}}$$

Velocity is simply how fast that position vector changes over time. Notice that velocity always points along the exact direction the object is traveling:

$$\mathbf{v}(t) = \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} = \dot{x}\hat{\mathbf{i}} + \dot{y}\hat{\mathbf{j}} + \dot{z}\hat{\mathbf{k}} = v \hat{\mathbf{t}}$$

where $v = |\mathbf{v}|$ is your speed, and $\hat{\mathbf{t}}$ is the unit vector pointing forward along the path.

Now, what happens when velocity changes? Acceleration has two completely separate duties:

$$\mathbf{a} = \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} = a_t \hat{\mathbf{t}} + a_n \hat{\mathbf{n}}$$
  • Tangential Acceleration ($a_t = \frac{dv}{dt}$): This is your speedometer. It only changes how fast you are moving. If speed is constant, $a_t = 0$.
  • Normal / Centripetal Acceleration ($a_n = \frac{v^2}{\rho}$): This is your steering wheel. It only changes your direction by bending the trajectory into a curve of radius $\rho$.

Total acceleration magnitude is the Pythagorean sum: $a = \sqrt{a_t^2 + a_n^2}$. If a car drives in a circle at constant speed, $a_t = 0$ but $a = a_n = v^2/R \ne 0$.

1.1.1 The Radius of Curvature Master Method

Think of the radius of curvature $\rho$ as the radius of the circle that best fits the curve at that exact point. To find it, use the simple relation between normal acceleration and speed:

$$a_\perp = \frac{v^2}{\rho} \implies \rho = \frac{v^2}{a_\perp}$$

Whenever you need $\rho$ on any trajectory:

  1. Find the speed $v = |\mathbf{v}|$.
  2. Find the net acceleration $\mathbf{a}$.
  3. Take the component of acceleration perpendicular to motion: $a_\perp = \sqrt{a^2 - a_\parallel^2}$.
  4. Compute $\rho = v^2 / a_\perp$.

If the trajectory is given as a formula $y = f(x)$, calculus gives:

$$\rho = \frac{\left[1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2\right]^{3/2}}{\left|\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\right|}$$

1.1.2 Projectile Motion on Inclined Planes

Let's demolish this classic exam topic. An inclined plane is tilted at angle $\beta$ to the horizontal. A projectile is launched from the incline with speed $u$ at an angle $\alpha$ relative to the incline surface.

The Mentor's Coordinate Hack: Don't use standard horizontal ($x$) and vertical ($y$) axes—that makes the ground boundary condition an annoying linear equation $y = x\tan\beta$. Instead, rotate your coordinate frame so the $x$-axis lies along the incline and the $y$-axis is perpendicular to the incline!

Now resolve acceleration due to gravity:

$$a_x = -g\sin\beta, \quad a_y = -g\cos\beta$$

Notice that gravity now acts along both axes! The flight ends when the projectile hits the incline, which is simply $y = 0$. That gives the time of flight instantly:

$$y(T) = (u\sin\alpha)T - \frac{1}{2}(g\cos\beta)T^2 = 0 \implies T = \frac{2u\sin\alpha}{g\cos\beta}$$

Range along the incline is the displacement along $x$ over this time $T$:

$$R = (u\cos\alpha)T - \frac{1}{2}(g\sin\beta)T^2 = \frac{u^2}{g\cos^2\beta}\left[\sin(2\alpha + \beta) - \sin\beta\right]$$

To maximize range up the incline, maximize $\sin(2\alpha + \beta) = 1 \implies 2\alpha + \beta = 90^\circ$:

$$\alpha_{\text{optimal}} = \frac{\pi}{4} - \frac{\beta}{2} = 45^\circ - \frac{\beta}{2}$$ $$R_{\max} = \frac{u^2}{g(1 + \sin\beta)}$$

For a downhill projectile, simply flip the sign of $\beta$: $R_{\max, \text{down}} = \frac{u^2}{g(1 - \sin\beta)}$.

1.1.3 River-Swimmer Optimal Drift Calculus & 3D Vector Interception

Let us dissect the river-swimmer problem with vector precision. Let river velocity be $\mathbf{v}_r = u\hat{i}$ and swimmer speed in still water be $v$. The width of the river is $d$. The swimmer heads at angle $\theta$ relative to the upstream bank.

Case 1: Crossing in Minimum Time:

The time to cross depends strictly on the perpendicular velocity component $v_y = v\sin\theta$:

$$t = \frac{d}{v\sin\theta}$$

To minimize $t$, maximize $\sin\theta = 1 \implies \theta = 90^\circ$ (swimming directly perpendicular to the bank). The minimum crossing time is $t_{\min} = \frac{d}{v}$. During this time, the swimmer drifts downstream by distance $x = u \cdot t_{\min} = \frac{u}{v}d$.

Case 2: Zero Drift Condition ($v \ge u$):

If the swimmer can swim faster than the river current, zero drift ($x = 0$) is possible by heading upstream such that the upstream velocity component cancels river flow:

$$v\cos\phi = u \implies \cos\phi = \frac{u}{v}$$

where $\phi$ is the angle with the upstream direction. The resulting crossing speed is $\sqrt{v^2 - u^2}$, and crossing time is $t = \frac{d}{\sqrt{v^2 - u^2}}$.

Case 3: Drift Minimization When River Outruns Swimmer ($u > v$):

This is where 80% of JEE aspirants lose marks. When $u > v$, zero drift is physically impossible—the current will inevitably sweep the swimmer downstream. The question becomes: What heading angle $\theta$ minimizes the total downstream drift $x$?

Let the swimmer head at angle $\theta$ to the upstream bank ($90^\circ < \theta < 180^\circ$). The crossing time is $t = \frac{d}{v\sin\theta}$, and the net downstream drift is:

$$x = (u - v\cos\theta)t = (u - v\cos\theta)\frac{d}{v\sin\theta} = d\left(\frac{u}{v}\csc\theta - \cot\theta\right)$$

To minimize drift, differentiate $x$ with respect to $\theta$ and set to zero:

$$\frac{dx}{d\theta} = d\left(-\frac{u}{v}\csc\theta\cot\theta + \csc^2\theta\right) = 0 \implies \cos\theta = \frac{v}{u}$$

Look at that magnificent result: when $u > v$, the optimal heading satisfies $\cos\theta = \mathbf{v/u}$, and the minimum downstream drift is:

$$x_{\min} = d \sqrt{\frac{u^2}{v^2} - 1}$$

The Geometric Tangent Construction: Draw a circle of radius $v$ centered at the origin, with vector $\mathbf{u}$ along the horizontal axis. The minimum drift occurs when the resultant velocity vector $\mathbf{v}_{res} = \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$ is tangent to the circle of radius $v$! This pure geometric construction yields $\sin\alpha = v/u$ in two seconds flat without calculus.

1.3 The Forensic Negative-Marking Trap Matrix (10 Alerts)

[TRAP ALERT 1.1] Average Speed vs. Magnitude of Average Velocity

The Trap: Assuming average speed $\langle v \rangle$ equals the magnitude of average velocity $|\langle \mathbf{v} \rangle|$.

The Definitive Rule: Average speed is Total Distance / Total Time: $\langle v \rangle = \frac{\int v dt}{\Delta t}$. Average velocity is Net Displacement / Total Time: $\langle \mathbf{v} \rangle = \frac{\Delta \mathbf{r}}{\Delta t}$. Because straight-line distance is always $\ge$ net displacement, $\langle v \rangle \ge |\langle \mathbf{v} \rangle|$. They are equal if and only if the particle moves in a straight line without reversing direction!
[TRAP ALERT 1.2] Zero Velocity Does NOT Mean Zero Acceleration

The Trap: Setting acceleration to zero when a particle momentarily comes to rest ($v = 0$).

The Definitive Rule: Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity ($a = dv/dt$), not velocity itself. At the apex of a vertical toss, $v = 0$, but acceleration is $g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$ downward! If acceleration were zero at the apex, the ball would freeze in mid-air forever.
[TRAP ALERT 1.3] Incline Projectile Angle Referencing Blunder

The Trap: Blindly using launch angle $\theta$ in formulas without checking whether the problem defines it relative to the horizontal ground or relative to the inclined plane surface.

The Definitive Rule: Always re-sketch and confirm: if launch angle from horizontal is $\theta$ and incline angle is $\beta$, the launch angle relative to the incline is $\alpha = \theta - \beta$. If you confuse these two angles, your range formula will be completely wrong.
[TRAP ALERT 1.4] Radius of Curvature Acceleration Projection Error

The Trap: Computing $\rho = v^2 / a$ using the total acceleration $a$ instead of the normal acceleration component $a_\perp$.

The Definitive Rule: By definition, curvature reflects trajectory bending, which is governed strictly by $a_\perp = v^2/\rho$. For a standard projectile at launch with speed $u$ at angle $\theta$, gravity acts straight down ($g$). Velocity is at angle $\theta$. The normal component of gravity is $a_\perp = g\cos\theta$. Therefore, $\rho = \frac{u^2}{g\cos\theta}$, NOT $u^2/g$!
[TRAP ALERT 1.5] River-Swimmer Drift Minimization when River Outruns Swimmer

The Trap: When river flow $u$ exceeds swimmer still-water speed $v$, students try to swim straight across or aim upstream at $\sin\theta = u/v > 1$, which is mathematically impossible.

The Definitive Rule: When $u > v$, drift cannot be zero. Draw the velocity circle: the tangent from the origin to the circle of radius $v$ centered at $(\mathbf{u})$ gives the minimum drift direction: $\sin\theta_{\min} = v/u$. The swimmer must aim upstream at angle $\theta = \arcsin(v/u)$ to the bank to minimize total downstream drift!
[TRAP ALERT 1.6] Rain-Man Relative Velocity Vector Sign Inversion

The Trap: Drawing the relative rain velocity as $\mathbf{v}_{rain} + \mathbf{v}_{man}$ instead of $\mathbf{v}_{rain} - \mathbf{v}_{man}$.

The Definitive Rule: The umbrella must point along the velocity of rain relative to the person: $\mathbf{v}_{r/m} = \mathbf{v}_r - \mathbf{v}_m$. If rain falls vertically with $-u\hat{\mathbf{j}}$ and you walk forward with $+v\hat{\mathbf{i}}$, then $\mathbf{v}_{r/m} = -v\hat{\mathbf{i}} - u\hat{\mathbf{j}}$. The rain appears to come from ahead of you, so you must tilt the umbrella forward!
[TRAP ALERT 1.7] Turning Point vs. Peak Height Identification

The Trap: Assuming a particle turns around only when position reaches a maximum, without checking for velocity sign change.

The Definitive Rule: A physical turning point occurs strictly where velocity changes sign: $\dot{x}(t_0) = 0$ with $\ddot{x}(t_0) \ne 0$. If velocity touches zero but doesn't change sign (e.g., $v(t) = t^2$), the particle merely pauses and continues in the same direction!
[TRAP ALERT 1.8] Misapplying Constant Acceleration to Non-Uniform Jerk

The Trap: Given acceleration $a = 2t$, calculating distance as $s = ut + \frac{1}{2}(2t)t^2 = ut + t^3$.

The Definitive Rule: The formula $\frac{1}{2}at^2$ is derived by integrating a constant acceleration twice. When $a(t) = 2t$, you must integrate from scratch: $v(t) = \int 2t dt = t^2 + v_0$, then $s(t) = \int (t^2 + v_0) dt = \frac{1}{3}t^3 + v_0 t$. The factor is $1/3$, not $1/2$ or $1$!
[TRAP ALERT 1.9] Projectile Velocity at Specified Height Has Two Solutions

The Trap: Assuming there is only one time and velocity when a projectile reaches height $h$.

The Definitive Rule: Gravity acts symmetrically. The projectile crosses height $h$ twice: once on the way up with velocity $(u_x \hat{\mathbf{i}} + v_y \hat{\mathbf{j}})$, and once on the way down with velocity $(u_x \hat{\mathbf{i}} - v_y \hat{\mathbf{j}})$. Their speeds are identical ($v = \sqrt{u^2 - 2gh}$), but their velocity vectors are completely different!
[TRAP ALERT 1.10] Horizontal Component of Velocity in Projectile Motion

The Trap: Forgetting that air drag or incline coordinate frames change the horizontal acceleration.

The Definitive Rule: Horizontal velocity is constant ($u_x = u\cos\theta$) if and only if there is zero horizontal force. In an inclined coordinate system, $a_x = -g\sin\beta \ne 0$. In real air drag, $a_x = -k v^2 \cos\theta \ne 0$. Always verify the net force along each axis before assuming velocity is constant!

Let the river flow along the positive $x$-axis: $\mathbf{u}_r = u\hat{\mathbf{i}} = 5\hat{\mathbf{i}}\text{ m/s}$.

$$\mathbf{v}_{swimmer} = (u - v\cos\theta)\hat{\mathbf{i}} + (v\sin\theta)\hat{\mathbf{j}}$$

Time to cross the river of width $d = 400\text{ m}$ is determined purely by the $y$-component:

$$t = \frac{d}{v_y} = \frac{d}{v\sin\theta}$$

The downstream drift $x$ is the horizontal distance traveled during this time:

$$x(\theta) = v_x t = (u - v\cos\theta)\left(\frac{d}{v\sin\theta}\right) = d\left(\frac{u}{v}\csc\theta - \cot\theta\right)$$

Differentiating $x(\theta)$ with respect to $\theta$ and setting to zero:

$$\frac{dx}{d\theta} = d\left(-\frac{u}{v}\csc\theta\cot\theta + \csc^2\theta\right) = 0$$

$$\cos\theta = \frac{v}{u}$$

Substituting swimmer speed $v = 3\text{ m/s}$ and river speed $u = 5\text{ m/s}$:

$$\cos\theta = \frac{3}{5} \implies \sin\theta = \frac{4}{5}$$ $$\theta = \arccos(3/5) \approx 53.13^\circ \quad (\text{directed upstream})$$

Given $x(t)$ has constant velocity: $\dot{x} = 4\text{ m/s} \implies \ddot{x} = 0$.

$$y = \alpha x^2 \implies \dot{y} = 2\alpha x \dot{x} = 2(0.5)(1)(4) = \mathbf{4\text{ m/s}}$$ $$\ddot{y} = \frac{d}{dt}(2\alpha x \dot{x}) = 2\alpha (\dot{x}^2 + x\ddot{x}) = 2(0.5)(4^2 + 1 \times 0) = \mathbf{16\text{ m/s}^2}$$

[MENTOR STRATEGY NOTE] The Reality of Friction in JEE Advanced

Listen to me very carefully: More students lose marks in friction than almost any other Class 11 mechanics topic. Why? Because you've been conditioned to write $f_s = \mu_s N$ the moment you see a block on a surface. That formula is not static friction—it is only the maximum possible breaking threshold! Static friction is a smart, self-adjusting reaction force. It only gives the exact amount needed to prevent slipping, nothing more.

The Golden Rule for Multi-Block Problems: Never guess which surface slips first. Assume all blocks move together as a single composite mass, compute the required acceleration, and check if any interface exceeds its friction ceiling. If one breaks, that's your first slipping surface!

2.1 First-Principles Foundations: Inertial Frames & Friction Hierarchies

Newton's Second Law ($\sum \mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$) is valid only in an inertial reference frame (a frame that is unaccelerated relative to the distant stars). When you decide to solve a problem from the perspective of an accelerating frame $S'$ (like a wedge sliding down an incline or an elevator with snapping cables), Newton's Second Law breaks unless you pay a mathematical tax: the pseudo-force.

$$\mathbf{F}_{\text{pseudo}} = -m \mathbf{a}_0$$

where $\mathbf{a}_0$ is the acceleration of your reference frame relative to the ground. Notice that minus sign! It means the pseudo-force always acts strictly opposite to the frame's acceleration vector. It acts uniformly on every atom, so its line of action passes directly through the Center of Mass (COM).

2.1.1 The Multi-Block Friction Hierarchy Algorithm

Consider two blocks stacked on top of each other: block $A$ of mass $m_A$ resting on block $B$ of mass $m_B$, which sits on a frictionless floor. A horizontal force $F$ is applied to block $B$. The coefficient of static friction between $A$ and $B$ is $\mu_s$.

Figure 2.1: Two-block stacked system. Force applied to bottom block B. Friction on A is the sole driving force for A.

Here is the 3-step foolproof algorithm to solve this in under 60 seconds:

  1. Find the Maximum Acceleration Block A Can Sustain Without Slipping: What accelerates block $A$? Only static friction from block $B$! Gravity and normal reaction are vertical. $$f_{s,\max} = \mu_s m_A g \implies a_{A,\max} = \frac{f_{s,\max}}{m_A} = \mathbf{\mu_s g}$$ Notice something incredible: the maximum acceleration block $A$ can ever have is purely $\mu_s g$, completely independent of its mass!
  2. Compute the Common Critical Force $F_c$: If both blocks accelerate together at this threshold acceleration $a_{A,\max}$, the required pulling force on the composite system $(m_A + m_B)$ is: $$F_c = (m_A + m_B) a_{A,\max} = \mathbf{(m_A + m_B)\mu_s g}$$
  3. Apply the Decision Tree:
    • If $F \le F_c$: No slipping occurs. Both blocks move together with common acceleration: $$a_A = a_B = \frac{F}{m_A + m_B}$$ The actual friction acting between them is: $f_s = m_A a_A = \frac{m_A}{m_A + m_B}F < \mu_s m_A g$.
    • If $F > F_c$: Relative slipping occurs! Kinetic friction $f_k = \mu_k m_A g$ acts between the blocks: $$a_A = \frac{f_k}{m_A} = \mu_k g = \text{constant}$$ $$a_B = \frac{F - f_k}{m_B} = \frac{F - \mu_k m_A g}{m_B}$$ The relative acceleration is $a_{rel} = a_B - a_A$.

2.1.2 Wedge-Block Kinematic Constraints & String Differentiation

In multi-body constrained mechanics, bodies cannot penetrate each other or pull away while remaining in contact. This geometric restriction gives rise to kinematic constraint relations that provide the vital missing algebraic equations needed to match the number of unknown dynamic variables.

The Virtual Work Method for Pulley Systems:

For any system of ideal massless inextensible strings connecting blocks through light frictionless pulleys, the tension forces are internal to the string-pulley network. Since the length of an inextensible string is strictly constant, the total virtual work done by all string tensions during any virtual displacement is identically zero:

$$\sum \mathbf{T}_i \cdot \Delta \mathbf{r}_i = 0 \implies \sum \mathbf{T}_i \cdot \mathbf{v}_i = 0 \implies \sum \mathbf{T}_i \cdot \mathbf{a}_i = 0$$

This single dot-product relation allows you to establish the exact ratio of block accelerations across any multi-pulley cascade without ever setting up laborious coordinate origin systems!

Wedge-Block Surface Normals:

When a block $A$ slides on a movable wedge $B$, their relative displacement must lie strictly parallel to the inclined contact face. If the wedge accelerates horizontally with acceleration $A_B$ to the left, the normal acceleration constraint between them requires that the velocity and acceleration components perpendicular to the contact interface must match perfectly:

$$(\mathbf{a}_A - \mathbf{a}_B) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}} = 0$$

Choosing coordinate axes aligned with the incline face immediately simplifies the equations of motion in the non-inertial frame of the wedge by confining relative motion to a single axis.

2.3 The Forensic Negative-Marking Trap Matrix (10 Alerts)

[TRAP ALERT 2.1] The $f_s = \mu_s N$ Knee-Jerk Fallacy

The Trap: A 5 kg block sits on a rough table with $\mu_s = 0.5$. A horizontal force of $F = 10\text{ N}$ is applied. Students compute friction as $f = \mu_s N = 0.5 \times 50 = 25\text{ N}$ and conclude the block accelerates backward!

The Definitive Rule: Static friction NEVER creates motion from rest. The value $\mu_s N = 25\text{ N}$ is the maximum threshold. Because applied force $F = 10\text{ N} < 25\text{ N}$, static friction adjusts to exactly balance the applied force: $f_s = 10\text{ N}$, and the block remains in static equilibrium ($a = 0$).
[TRAP ALERT 2.2] Friction Opposes Motion vs. Friction Opposes RELATIVE Motion

The Trap: Assuming friction always opposes the velocity of an object relative to the ground.

The Definitive Rule: Friction opposes relative slipping between contact surfaces, NOT ground motion! When you walk, friction from the ground pushes you FORWARD. When block $A$ sits on accelerating block $B$, friction on $A$ acts FORWARD to accelerate it. If friction opposed ground motion, walking would be physically impossible.
[TRAP ALERT 2.3] Normal Force on an Accelerating Incline

The Trap: Assuming normal force on an inclined plane is always $N = mg\cos\theta$, even when the wedge is accelerating horizontally with acceleration $a_0$.

The Definitive Rule: In the non-inertial frame of the wedge accelerating rightward, a pseudo-force $m a_0$ acts leftward. Perpendicular force balance yields: $N = mg\cos\theta + m a_0\sin\theta$. Normal force increases or decreases depending on the direction of wedge acceleration!
[TRAP ALERT 2.4] Tension Discontinuity Across Massive Pulleys

The Trap: Assuming string tension is identical on both sides of a massive or rough pulley ($T_1 = T_2$).

The Definitive Rule: If a pulley has mass $M$ and radius $R$ (moment of inertia $I$), net torque is required to give it angular acceleration: $(T_1 - T_2)R = I\alpha$. Therefore, $T_1 \ne T_2$! Tension is constant across a pulley if and only if the pulley is strictly massless and frictionless.
[TRAP ALERT 2.5] Contact Separation Criterion ($N = 0$)

The Trap: Attempting to find when a block flies off a curved surface or wedge by setting acceleration to zero.

The Definitive Rule: Contact loss between rigid bodies is governed strictly by the normal reaction vanishing: $N \to 0$. Surfaces can only push, they cannot pull ($N \ge 0$). The moment $N = 0$, the body detaches and enters free projectile flight.
[TRAP ALERT 2.6] Pseudo-Force in Rotating Frames

The Trap: Using only the centrifugal pseudo-force $-m(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{r}))$ when analyzing a particle moving inside a rotating reference frame.

The Definitive Rule: If a particle moves with relative velocity $\mathbf{v}_{rel}$ inside a rotating frame, you MUST include the Coriolis Force: $\mathbf{F}_{\text{coriolis}} = -2m(\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\mathbf{v}_{rel})$. Neglecting Coriolis leads to massive errors in Bead-on-Rotating-Rod problems.
[TRAP ALERT 2.7] Virtual Work String Constraint Differentiation Signs

The Trap: Confusing positive and negative signs when applying $\sum \mathbf{T}\cdot\mathbf{a} = 0$ to complex multi-pulley systems.

The Definitive Rule: Choose an unambiguous positive direction for each block. Compute the work done by internal tension: $\sum T_i x_i = 0$. Differentiating twice yields $\sum T_i a_i = 0$. If tension and acceleration are in the same direction, the dot product is positive; if opposite, it is negative.
[TRAP ALERT 2.8] Conical Pendulum Critical Speed Limit

The Trap: Assuming a conical pendulum can spin at any arbitrary slow angular velocity $\omega$ and still maintain a conical orbit.

The Definitive Rule: Tension components give $T\cos\theta = mg$ and $T\sin\theta = m\omega^2 L\sin\theta$. This requires $\cos\theta = \frac{g}{\omega^2 L}$. Because $\cos\theta \le 1$, a conical orbit is physically impossible unless $\omega > \sqrt{g/L}$. For $\omega \le \sqrt{g/L}$, the string hangs purely vertically ($\theta = 0$).
[TRAP ALERT 2.9] Kinetic Friction Velocity Independence

The Trap: Assuming kinetic friction increases drastically as sliding velocity increases.

The Definitive Rule: In Coulomb's model for JEE, $f_k = \mu_k N$ is independent of sliding speed and apparent contact area. It remains constant whether sliding at $1\text{ m/s}$ or $10\text{ m/s}$ (neglecting aerodynamic heating).
[TRAP ALERT 2.10] Equilibrium Under Three Non-Parallel Forces

The Trap: Balancing three non-parallel forces on a rigid body without checking whether their lines of action intersect at a single common point.

The Definitive Rule: For a rigid body to be in rotational equilibrium ($\sum \boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{0}$) under three non-parallel coplanar forces, the three force vectors MUST be concurrent—their lines of action must intersect at the exact same spatial point!

JEE Advanced 2020 • Paper 1 Difficulty: Very Hard • Three-Block Friction Stack Hierarchy
Three blocks $A, B,$ and $C$ of masses $m_A = 1\text{ kg}, m_B = 2\text{ kg},$ and $m_C = 3\text{ kg}$ are stacked on top of each other. $A$ sits on $B$, $B$ sits on $C$, and $C$ rests on a frictionless horizontal floor. The coefficient of static friction between $A$ and $B$ is $\mu_1 = 0.6$, and between $B$ and $C$ is $\mu_2 = 0.2$. A horizontal force $F$ is applied to the middle block $B$. Find (a) the maximum force $F_{\max}$ such that all three blocks move together without slipping, and (b) the accelerations of all three blocks when $F = 30\text{ N}$. Take $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$.
First-Principles Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Determine Maximum Acceleration for Each Interface:

For Block $A$ (driven purely by friction from $B$):

$$f_{1,\max} = \mu_1 m_A g = 0.6(1)(10) = 6\text{ N} \implies a_{A,\max} = \frac{f_{1,\max}}{m_A} = \frac{6}{1} = \mathbf{6\text{ m/s}^2}$$

For Block $C$ (driven purely by friction from $B$):

Normal force on interface between $B$ and $C$ carries mass of $A$ and $B$: $N_2 = (m_A + m_B)g = (1 + 2)(10) = 30\text{ N}$.

$$f_{2,\max} = \mu_2 N_2 = 0.2(30) = 6\text{ N}$$

Block $C$ has mass $m_C = 3\text{ kg}$. The maximum acceleration friction can impart to $C$ is:

$$a_{C,\max} = \frac{f_{2,\max}}{m_C} = \frac{6}{3} = \mathbf{2\text{ m/s}^2}$$

Step 2: Find Weakest Link for Co-Movement:

Notice that $a_{C,\max} = 2\text{ m/s}^2 < a_{A,\max} = 6\text{ m/s}^2$. Therefore, block $C$ is the weakest link! The interface between $B$ and $C$ will slip long before $A$ slips on $B$.

The maximum common acceleration for all three blocks to move together is $a_{\text{common}} = 2\text{ m/s}^2$.

$$F_{\max} = (m_A + m_B + m_C) a_{\text{common}} = (1 + 2 + 3)(2) = 6 \times 2 = \mathbf{12\text{ Newtons}}$$

Step 3: Analyze System at $F = 30\text{ N}$ ($F > 12\text{ N}$):

Because $F = 30\text{ N} > 12\text{ N}$, slipping occurs between $B$ and $C$. Kinetic friction acts on $C$:

$$f_2 = f_{2,\max} = 6\text{ N} \implies a_C = \frac{6}{3} = \mathbf{2.0\text{ m/s}^2}$$

Now check whether $A$ slips on $B$. Treat $(A + B)$ as a combined sub-system of mass $(1 + 2) = 3\text{ kg}$ subject to pulling force $F = 30\text{ N}$ forward and backward friction $f_2 = 6\text{ N}$ from $C$:

$$a_{AB} = \frac{F - f_2}{m_A + m_B} = \frac{30 - 6}{1 + 2} = \frac{24}{3} = 8\text{ m/s}^2$$

Compare $a_{AB} = 8\text{ m/s}^2$ with $a_{A,\max} = 6\text{ m/s}^2$. Since $8 > 6$, Block $A$ ALSO slips on Block $B$!

Therefore, kinetic friction acts at both interfaces: $f_1 = 6\text{ N}$ and $f_2 = 6\text{ N}$.

$$a_A = \frac{f_1}{m_A} = \frac{6}{1} = \mathbf{6.0\text{ m/s}^2}$$ $$a_B = \frac{F - f_1 - f_2}{m_B} = \frac{30 - 6 - 6}{2} = \frac{18}{2} = \mathbf{9.0\text{ m/s}^2}$$ $$a_C = \frac{f_2}{m_C} = \frac{6}{3} = \mathbf{2.0\text{ m/s}^2}$$

A block of mass $m = 1\text{ kg}$ is placed on the inclined face of the wedge and released from rest. Determine (a) the horizontal acceleration $A$ of the wedge, and (b) the acceleration $a_{rel}$ of the block relative to the wedge. First-Principles Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Isolate Wedge in the Laboratory Frame:

The only horizontal force acting on the wedge is the horizontal component of the normal reaction $N$ from the block directed leftward:

$$N\sin\theta = M A \implies N = \frac{M A}{\sin\theta}$$

Step 2: Analyze Block in Wedge Reference Frame (Accelerating Leftward at $A$):

In this non-inertial frame, a pseudo-force $m A$ acts rightward on the block.

Because the block remains in contact with the incline, acceleration perpendicular to the incline is zero:

$$N + m A \sin\theta = m g \cos\theta \implies N = m g \cos\theta - m A \sin\theta$$

$$\frac{M A}{\sin\theta} = m g \cos\theta - m A \sin\theta$$ $$M A = m g \sin\theta\cos\theta - m A \sin^2\theta \implies A(M + m\sin^2\theta) = m g \sin\theta\cos\theta$$ $$A = \frac{m g \sin\theta\cos\theta}{M + m\sin^2\theta}$$

Forces along the incline in the wedge frame:

$$m a_{rel} = m g \sin\theta + m A \cos\theta \implies a_{rel} = g\sin\theta + A\cos\theta$$ $$a_{rel} = 10(0.6) + \left(\frac{120}{109}\right)(0.8) = 6 + \frac{96}{109} = \frac{654 + 96}{109} = \mathbf{\frac{750}{109}\text{ m/s}^2} \approx \mathbf{6.881\text{ m/s}^2}$$

Chapter 03

Work, Energy & Power Mastery

[MENTOR STRATEGY NOTE] Why Energy Methods Separate Top Ranks from the Rest

Listen closely, aspirants: Newton's Second Law ($\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$) is a vector differential equation. If the force direction changes continuously—like a bead sliding on a curved wire or a pendulum swinging through an arc—solving $\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$ directly requires solving painful differential equations. Energy methods bypass time and trajectory details completely. A single scalar equation connects your initial launch state directly to your final state.

The Catch? The Work-Energy Theorem ($W_{net} = \Delta K$) is foolproof, but students blunder constantly on the signs: work done by normal forces on moving wedges is NOT zero, string tension slackening is NOT velocity vanishing in vertical circles, and internal forces can do massive amounts of work. Let's master the physics so you never get trapped.

3.1 First-Principles Foundations: Energy Conservation & Field Theorems

Where does the Work-Energy Theorem come from? It is not a separate postulate—it is simply Newton's Second Law integrated along the path of motion:

$$W_{net} = \int_{1}^{2} \mathbf{F}_{net} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = m\int_{\mathbf{v}_1}^{\mathbf{v}_2} \mathbf{v}\cdot d\mathbf{v} = \frac{1}{2}m v_2^2 - \frac{1}{2}m v_1^2 = \Delta K$$

Look at what that derivation assumed: absolutely nothing about the nature of the forces! It holds true in any inertial frame for gravity, spring forces, friction, normal pushes, and tension. Total work done by all forces always equals the change in kinetic energy.

3.1.1 Potential Energy Curves $U(x)$ and Equilibrium Stability

Think of potential energy $U(x)$ like a rolling landscape. A ball naturally rolls downhill toward lower potential energy. The force pushing the object is simply how steep that hill is at that point, with a minus sign because the push points downhill toward lower energy: $F(x) = -\frac{dU}{dx}$. An equilibrium point $x_0$ is a flat spot—the bottom of a valley, the crest of a ridge, or a flat plateau—where the slope is zero and the net force vanishes: $\left.\frac{dU}{dx}\right|_{x_0} = 0$.

Figure 3.1: Potential energy landscape $U(x)$ indicating stable, unstable, and neutral equilibrium coordinates.

Classifying equilibrium points is straightforward:

  • Stable Equilibrium (Valley Bottom): $\left.\frac{d^2U}{dx^2}\right|_{x_0} > 0$. The curve cups upward. If you nudge the particle, the walls push it right back. The particle oscillates in Simple Harmonic Motion with frequency: $$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{U''(x_0)}{m}}$$
  • Unstable Equilibrium (Hill Crest): $\left.\frac{d^2U}{dx^2}\right|_{x_0} < 0$. The curve domes downward. A tiny nudge rolls the particle further away down the slope.
  • Neutral Equilibrium (Flat Plateau): $\left.\frac{d^2U}{dx^2}\right|_{x_0} = 0$. The ground is flat. Nudging the particle simply moves it to a new resting spot with no restoring or runaway force.

3.1.2 Vertical Circular Motion (VCM): The Definitive Regime Breakdown

Take a bob of mass $m$ tied to a light string of length $R$ and project it horizontally at the lowest point with speed $u$. Let $\theta$ be the angle from the downward vertical. Mechanical energy conservation gives velocity at any angle:

$$v^2(\theta) = u^2 - 2gR(1 - \cos\theta)$$

Now write Newton's Second Law along the radial direction toward the center:

$$T(\theta) - mg\cos\theta = \frac{mv^2(\theta)}{R} \implies T(\theta) = \frac{m}{R}\left[u^2 - gR(2 - 3\cos\theta)\right]$$

Now memorize these three distinct physical regimes—JEE Advanced tests the boundaries every year:

  1. The Pure Oscillation Regime ($u \le \sqrt{2gR}$): Velocity vanishes ($v = 0$) at or before $\theta = 90^\circ$ (horizontal level). Because $\cos\theta \ge 0$, tension $T = \frac{m}{R}[u^2 - gR(2 - 3\cos\theta)]$ remains strictly positive throughout! The string never goes slack. The bob oscillates like a regular pendulum with maximum angular amplitude $\theta_{\max} = \arccos(1 - u^2/2gR)$.
  2. The Slackening & Projectile Regime ($\sqrt{2gR} < u < \sqrt{5gR}$): Velocity is still positive at $\theta = 90^\circ$, but tension vanishes ($T = 0$) at an angle $\theta_0 \in (90^\circ, 180^\circ)$ in the upper semicircle! Setting $T(\theta_0) = 0$: $$\cos\theta_0 = -\frac{u^2 - 2gR}{3gR}$$ Fatal Mistake to Avoid: The particle does NOT fall straight down! At the instant $T=0$, the string goes limp, but the particle has speed $v_0 = \sqrt{gR|\cos\theta_0|}$ directed tangentially upward. From this instant, the particle acts as a free parabolic projectile under gravity alone!
  3. The Complete Looping Regime ($u \ge \sqrt{5gR}$): For complete vertical circular loops, tension must not vanish even at the very top ($\theta = 180^\circ$): $$T_{top} = \frac{m}{R}(u^2 - 5gR) \ge 0 \implies u \ge \sqrt{5gR}$$ At critical speed $u = \sqrt{5gR}$, the speed at the top is $v_{top} = \sqrt{gR}$, and tension at the top is exactly zero. Notice this universal, launch-speed-independent identity: $$T_{bottom} - T_{top} = 6mg$$

3.1.3 Potential Energy Landscape & Turning Point Phase Analysis

When a particle of total mechanical energy $E$ moves in a conservative one-dimensional potential field $U(x)$, its kinetic energy is strictly non-negative: $K(x) = E - U(x) \ge 0$. This fundamental thermodynamic and kinematic requirement partitions spatial geometry into allowed and forbidden physical zones:

  • Classically Allowed Region: The domain where $E \ge U(x)$. Here velocity is real: $v(x) = \pm \sqrt{\frac{2(E - U(x))}{m}}$.
  • Turning Points: The boundary coordinates $x_t$ where total energy equals potential energy: $U(x_t) = E$. At these points, kinetic energy drops to zero ($K = 0$), so velocity vanishes momentarily ($v = 0$). The particle reflects back into the potential well.
  • Classically Forbidden Region: Any zone where $E < U(x)$. In classical mechanics, the particle can never penetrate this barrier because its kinetic energy would be negative, yielding imaginary velocity.

Bounded vs. Unbounded Orbits:

If the particle is trapped between two turning points $x_1$ and $x_2$ such that $U(x_1) = U(x_2) = E$ and $U(x) < E$ for all $x \in (x_1, x_2)$, the particle undergoes periodic oscillatory motion with period given by the path action integral:

$$T = 2 \int_{x_1}^{x_2} \frac{dx}{v(x)} = 2\sqrt{\frac{m}{2}} \int_{x_1}^{x_2} \frac{dx}{\sqrt{E - U(x)}}$$

Evaluating this integral yields the exact time period for arbitrary non-linear potentials where small-angle harmonic approximations no longer hold!

3.3 10 Forensic NTA/JEE Negative-Marking Trap Alerts

[TRAP ALERT 3.1] The "Normal Force Never Does Work" Dogma

The Trap: Believing blindly that normal contact force can never do work because it is perpendicular to the contact surface.

Definitive Rule: Normal force does zero work only if the contact surface is stationary in your reference frame! In a movable wedge system, as the block slides down, the normal force on the wedge pushes it horizontally in the direction of motion ($W_N > 0$ on wedge), while doing equal negative work on the block ($W_N < 0$ on block). The net work summed over the closed rigid system is zero ($\sum W_N = 0$), but on individual components, normal force transfers mechanical energy!
[TRAP ALERT 3.2] String Slackening vs Velocity Vanishing in VCM

The Trap: When a bob launched with $u = \sqrt{3gR}$ enters vertical circular motion, setting $v = 0$ to find the peak elevation.

Definitive Rule: For $\sqrt{2gR} < u < \sqrt{5gR}$, tension dies first ($T = 0$) while velocity is still non-zero ($v_0 = \sqrt{gR/3}$). The bob does not stop—it leaves the circle and flies as a free projectile under gravity! Peak height is $h = h_{slack} + \frac{v_{0y}^2}{2g}$.
[TRAP ALERT 3.3] Reference Frame Relativity of Work and Kinetic Energy

The Trap: Assuming work done by a force is an absolute scalar invariant that has the same numerical value in all reference frames.

Definitive Rule: Displacement is frame-dependent: $d\mathbf{r} = d\mathbf{r}' + \mathbf{V}_0 dt$. Therefore, work done $W = \int \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$ and kinetic energy are strictly frame-dependent! However, the Work-Energy Theorem $\Delta K = W_{net}$ holds true in every single inertial frame.
[TRAP ALERT 3.4] The Spring Potential Energy Sign Blunder

The Trap: Writing spring potential energy as negative $-\frac{1}{2}kx^2$ by false analogy with gravitational potential energy $-GMm/r$.

Definitive Rule: Work done by the spring force on a block is $W_s = -\Delta U_s = -\frac{1}{2}k(x_2^2 - x_1^2)$. The elastic potential energy stored in an elongated or compressed spring is strictly positive: $U_s = +\frac{1}{2}kx^2 \ge 0$.
[TRAP ALERT 3.5] VCM Looping with a Rigid Rod vs Light String

The Trap: Applying $u = \sqrt{5gR}$ to determine the minimum launch speed to complete a vertical loop for a mass fixed to a light rigid rod.

Definitive Rule: A rigid rod can support compression ($N < 0$) without collapsing or slacking. To loop, velocity at the top only needs to not become imaginary: $v_{top} \ge 0$. By energy conservation, $u_{\min} = \sqrt{4gR} = 2\sqrt{gR}$, NOT $\sqrt{5gR}$!
[TRAP ALERT 3.6] The 50% Thermal Dissipation Paradox on Conveyor Belts

The Trap: Dropping a mass $m$ gently onto a conveyor belt moving at speed $v$. Students compute work done on the mass as $\frac{1}{2}mv^2$ and claim the motor supplied only $\frac{1}{2}mv^2$.

Definitive Rule: During the slipping phase, the belt travels exactly twice the distance that the block slides across the floor! Work done by the motor is $W_{motor} = mv^2$. The block gains kinetic energy $K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$, and exactly $50\%$ of the energy ($Q = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$) is dissipated as thermal friction heat, universally independent of $\mu$!
[TRAP ALERT 3.7] Integrating Potential to Find Force (Upside Down Calculus)

The Trap: Given $U(x,y) = x^2 y$, finding the force component as $F_x = -\int U dx$.

Definitive Rule: Force is the negative spatial derivative (gradient): $\mathbf{F} = -\nabla U = -\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\hat{i} + \frac{\partial U}{\partial y}\hat{j}\right) = -2xy\hat{i} - x^2\hat{j}$. Never integrate a potential function to find force.
[TRAP ALERT 3.8] Forgetting Pseudo-Work in Accelerating Frames

The Trap: Applying the work-energy theorem in an accelerating frame without accounting for the work done by pseudo-forces.

Definitive Rule: In an accelerating frame $S'$ with acceleration $\mathbf{a}_0$, you must include the work done by the pseudo-force: $W_{real} + W_{pseudo} = \Delta K'$, where $W_{pseudo} = \int (-m\mathbf{a}_0) \cdot d\mathbf{r}'$.
[TRAP ALERT 3.9] Constant Power Engine Velocity Singularity at Launch

The Trap: Integrating $P = Fv \implies v = (3Pt/m)^{1/3}$ to calculate launch time from rest, ignoring that at $t = 0$, force would be infinite ($F = P/0$).

Definitive Rule: In real vehicles, force is limited by tire friction: $F_{\max} = \mu mg$. The vehicle accelerates at constant acceleration $a = \mu g$ until it reaches critical transition speed $v_c = P/(\mu mg)$, and only then transitions to constant power mode!
[TRAP ALERT 3.10] Internal Conservative Forces Doing Non-Zero Work

The Trap: Claiming internal forces do zero work in every system because Newton's Third Law says $\sum \mathbf{F}_{int} = 0$.

Definitive Rule: $\sum \mathbf{F}_{int} = 0$ guarantees momentum conservation, NOT zero work! Internal forces do work whenever the relative distance between interacting particles changes: $W_{int} = -\Delta U_{internal}$. Only in a strictly rigid body is total internal work identically zero.

At slackening, velocity is tangential to the circle, inclined at angle $\phi = \theta_0 - 90^\circ$ above the horizontal. Therefore, $\sin\phi = \cos(\theta_0 - 90^\circ) = -\cos\theta_0 = \frac{1}{3}$.

Additional projectile height attained from slackening point:

$$\Delta h = \frac{v_{0y}^2}{2g} = \frac{(v_0\sin\phi)^2}{2g} = \frac{(gR/3)(1/9)}{2g} = \frac{R}{54} = \frac{1}{54}\text{ m}$$ $$H_{\max} = h_0 + \Delta h = \frac{4}{3} + \frac{1}{54} = \frac{72 + 1}{54} = \mathbf{\frac{73}{54}\text{ m}} \approx 1.352\text{ m}$$
[WORKED JEE ADVANCED PYQ] JEE Advanced 2014 • Paper 2 Difficulty: Moderate • Potential Well Equilibrium & Oscillation
A particle of mass $m = 0.2\text{ kg}$ moves along the $x$-axis in a conservative potential field given by $U(x) = \frac{a}{x^2} - \frac{b}{x}$, where $a = 2\text{ J}\cdot\text{m}^2$ and $b = 4\text{ J}\cdot\text{m}$. Find (a) the equilibrium position $x_0$, (b) the nature of equilibrium, (c) the minimum binding energy to escape to infinity, and (d) the time period of small oscillations about $x_0$.
First-Principles Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Locate Equilibrium Position ($dU/dx = 0$):

$$\frac{dU}{dx} = -\frac{2a}{x^3} + \frac{b}{x^2} = 0 \implies \frac{b}{x^2} = \frac{2a}{x^3} \implies x_0 = \frac{2a}{b} = \frac{2(2)}{4} = \mathbf{1\text{ m}}$$

Step 2: Second Derivative Test for Stability:

$$\frac{d^2U}{dx^2} = \frac{6a}{x^4} - \frac{2b}{x^3}$$ $$\left.\frac{d^2U}{dx^2}\right|_{x=1} = \frac{6(2)}{1^4} - \frac{2(4)}{1^3} = 12 - 8 = +4\text{ N/m} > 0 \implies \mathbf{\text{Stable Equilibrium}}$$

Step 3: Minimum Potential & Binding Energy:

$$U(x_0) = \frac{2}{1^2} - \frac{4}{1} = 2 - 4 = -2\text{ J}$$

As $x \to \infty$, $U(\infty) = 0$. The binding energy to overcome the well and reach infinity is $E_{bind} = |U(x_0)| = \mathbf{2\text{ J}}$.

Step 4: Small Oscillation Time Period:

$$k_{eff} = U''(x_0) = 4\text{ N/m}$$ $$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k_{eff}}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{4}{0.2}} = \sqrt{20} = 2\sqrt{5}\text{ rad/s}$$ $$T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = \frac{2\pi}{2\sqrt{5}} = \mathbf{\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{5}}\text{ s}} \approx 1.405\text{ seconds}$$
[WORKED JEE ADVANCED PYQ] JEE Advanced 2019 • Paper 2 Difficulty: Hard • Work Done by Moving Incline Boundary
A wedge of mass $M = 2\text{ kg}$ with smooth inclined face of angle $\alpha = 30^\circ$ sits on a smooth horizontal floor. A block of mass $m = 1\text{ kg}$ is released from rest at height $h = 1\text{ m}$ on the incline. Find the work done by the normal contact force on (a) the block and (b) the wedge during the descent to the floor. Take $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$.
First-Principles Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Mechanical Energy & Momentum Conservation:

Friction is zero everywhere, so total mechanical energy is conserved: $\Delta K_{block} + \Delta K_{wedge} = mgh = 1 \times 10 \times 1 = 10\text{ J}$.

Horizontal momentum is conserved: $m v_{bx} + M V_{wx} = 0 \implies v_{bx} = -\frac{M}{m}V_w = -2V_w$.

Kinematic constraint along incline: relative velocity gives $v_{by} = (v_{bx} - V_w)\tan 30^\circ = -3V_w / \sqrt{3} = -\sqrt{3}V_w$.

Step 2: Solve Kinetic Energies:

$$K_{wedge} = \frac{1}{2}M V_w^2 = \frac{1}{2}(2)V_w^2 = V_w^2$$ $$K_{block} = \frac{1}{2}m(v_{bx}^2 + v_{by}^2) = \frac{1}{2}(1)[(-2V_w)^2 + (-\sqrt{3}V_w)^2] = \frac{1}{2}(4 + 3)V_w^2 = 3.5 V_w^2$$ $$K_{wedge} + K_{block} = 10\text{ J} \implies 4.5 V_w^2 = 10 \implies V_w^2 = \frac{20}{9}$$ $$K_{wedge} = \frac{20}{9}\text{ J} \approx 2.22\text{ J}, \quad K_{block} = 3.5\left(\frac{20}{9}\right) = \frac{70}{9}\text{ J} \approx 7.78\text{ J}$$

Step 3: Work Done by Normal Force:

For the wedge: Normal force from the block is the sole agent doing work:

$$W_{N, \text{on wedge}} = \Delta K_{wedge} = \mathbf{+\frac{20}{9}\text{ J}} \approx +2.22\text{ J}$$

For the block: Gravity and normal force both act: $W_g + W_{N, \text{on block}} = \Delta K_{block}$

$$10 + W_{N, \text{on block}} = \frac{70}{9} \implies W_{N, \text{on block}} = \frac{70}{9} - 10 = \mathbf{-\frac{20}{9}\text{ J}} \approx -2.22\text{ J}$$

Fundamental Law Confirmed: $W_{N,\text{wedge}} + W_{N,\text{block}} = +\frac{20}{9} - \frac{20}{9} \equiv 0\text{ J}$. Normal contact forces between rigid bodies transfer mechanical energy across boundaries with zero net destruction!

Chapter 04

Center of Mass, Linear Momentum & Collisions

[MENTOR STRATEGY NOTE] The Mindset Shift: From Particles to Systems

Listen up, future IITians: Up until now, you've treated every object as a point mass. That luxury ends today. In the real world, bodies have shape, internal structure, and colliding boundaries. Center of mass (COM) is the single most powerful simplifying concept in mechanics—it is the point that moves as if the entire system's mass were concentrated there and all external forces were applied directly to it.

Where students lose marks in JEE Advanced: They apply the coefficient of restitution $e$ along the direction of motion instead of the normal Line of Impact (LOI). They forget that fixed hinges destroy linear momentum through massive impulsive reactions. And they butcher the relative velocity signs in variable-mass rocket equations. We are going to lock down every single one of these concepts right now.

4.1 First-Principles Foundations: Mass Distributions & Collision Dynamics

4.1.1 Continuous Mass Formulations & Standard Integrals

Think of the Center of Mass (COM) as the single balance point of the body. If you supported the object on the tip of a pin right at this point, it wouldn't tip in any direction. Mathematically, it is the weighted average position of all the mass in the system:

$$\mathbf{r}_{cm} = \frac{\sum m_i \mathbf{r}_i}{\sum m_i} = \frac{1}{M} \int \mathbf{r} \, dm$$

To save critical exam minutes, memorize these six standard geometries and how their centroids are derived:

  • Uniform Semicircular Thin Wire (Radius $R$): Take polar element $dm = \lambda R d\theta$, height $y = R\sin\theta$. $$y_{cm} = \frac{\int_0^\pi (R\sin\theta)(\lambda R d\theta)}{\int_0^\pi \lambda R d\theta} = \frac{R\int_0^\pi \sin\theta d\theta}{\pi} = \mathbf{\frac{2R}{\pi}} \approx 0.637 R$$
  • Uniform Semicircular Planar Disc (Radius $R$): Slice into concentric semicircular strips of radius $r$, area $dA = \pi r dr$, strip centroid at $2r/\pi$. $$y_{cm} = \frac{\int_0^R \left(\frac{2r}{\pi}\right)(\sigma \pi r dr)}{\frac{1}{2}\sigma \pi R^2} = \frac{2\int_0^R r^2 dr}{\frac{1}{2}\pi R^2} = \mathbf{\frac{4R}{3\pi}} \approx 0.424 R$$
  • Uniform Thin Hemispherical Shell (Radius $R$): Slice into rings at polar angle $\theta$, radius $R\sin\theta$, height $y = R\cos\theta$, area $dA = 2\pi R^2 \sin\theta d\theta$. $$y_{cm} = \frac{\int_0^{\pi/2} (R\cos\theta)(2\pi R^2 \sigma \sin\theta d\theta)}{2\pi R^2 \sigma} = R\int_0^{\pi/2} \sin\theta\cos\theta d\theta = \mathbf{\frac{R}{2}} = 0.500 R$$
  • Uniform Solid Hemisphere (Radius $R$): Sliced into flat discs at height $z \in [0, R]$ with radius $\sqrt{R^2 - z^2}$, mass $dm = \rho \pi (R^2 - z^2) dz$. $$z_{cm} = \frac{\int_0^R z \pi (R^2 - z^2) dz}{\frac{2}{3}\pi R^3} = \frac{\pi \left[ \frac{R^2 z^2}{2} - \frac{z^4}{4} \right]_0^R}{\frac{2}{3}\pi R^3} = \frac{\frac{1}{4}\pi R^4}{\frac{2}{3}\pi R^3} = \mathbf{\frac{3R}{8}} = 0.375 R$$
  • Uniform Hollow Cone (Height $h$): Sliced into elemental rings along the slant face: $y_{cm} = \mathbf{\frac{h}{3}}$ from the base.
  • Uniform Solid Cone (Height $h$): Sliced into circular discs along the vertical symmetry axis: $y_{cm} = \mathbf{\frac{h}{4}}$ from the base.

4.1.2 Center of Mass Frame (C-Frame) & Koenig's Kinetic Energy Theorem

The Center of Mass reference frame (zero-momentum frame) translates at velocity $\mathbf{V}_{cm} = \frac{\sum m_i \mathbf{v}_i}{\sum m_i}$. In this frame, the net linear momentum vanishes identically by definition:

$$\mathbf{P}_{cm}^{C\text{-frame}} = \sum m_i \mathbf{v}_i^* = \sum m_i (\mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{V}_{cm}) = \mathbf{P}_{lab} - M \mathbf{V}_{cm} \equiv \mathbf{0}$$

Koenig's Kinetic Energy Decomposition Theorem: The total kinetic energy in the laboratory frame decouples cleanly into the kinetic energy of the center of mass plus the internal kinetic energy relative to the center of mass:

$$K_{lab} = \frac{1}{2} M V_{cm}^2 + \sum \frac{1}{2} m_i (v_i^*)^2 = K_{cm} + K_{rel}$$

For any two-body system ($m_1, m_2$), introducing the reduced mass $\mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}$ collapses the relative kinetic energy to that of a single virtual particle:

$$K_{rel} = \frac{1}{2} \mu v_{rel}^2 \quad \text{where } \mathbf{v}_{rel} = \mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_2$$

When two colliding bodies coalesce or couple through a spring, maximum deformation occurs when relative velocity vanishes ($\mathbf{v}_{rel} = \mathbf{0}$). The kinetic energy converted to deformation or spring potential energy is exactly:

$$\Delta E_{\max} = K_{rel} = \frac{1}{2} \mu u_{rel}^2 = \frac{1}{2} \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2} (u_1 - u_2)^2$$

4.1.3 Oblique Collisions & Newton's Law of Restitution

During an impact of finite microsecond duration $\Delta t \to 0$, normal forces generate massive internal impulses $J = \int N dt \gg mg\Delta t$. Newton's experimental law of restitution governs the normal components of approach and separation along the Line of Impact (LOI):

$$e = \frac{v_{2n} - v_{1n}}{u_{1n} - u_{2n}} = \frac{\text{Relative Speed of Separation along LOI}}{\text{Relative Speed of Approach along LOI}}$$
  • Perfectly Elastic Collision ($e = 1$): Zero mechanical energy loss. Relative separation speed equals relative approach speed.
  • Inelastic Collision ($0 < e < 1$): Fractional loss of relative normal kinetic energy: $$\Delta K_{loss} = \frac{1}{2} \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2} (1 - e^2) (u_{1n} - u_{2n})^2$$
  • Perfectly Inelastic Collision ($e = 0$): Bodies attain identical velocity components along LOI ($v_{1n} = v_{2n}$). Maximum possible mechanical energy is converted to thermal and deformation energy.
  • Tangential Component Conservation: For smooth surfaces ($\mu = 0$), impulsive friction is zero ($J_t = 0$). Consequently, velocity components parallel to the Common Tangential Plane (COT) are strictly conserved: $$v_{1t} = u_{1t}, \quad v_{2t} = u_{2t}$$

4.1.4 Variable Mass Dynamics: Rocketry & Control Volume Mechanics

For an open system with mass entering or exiting at rate $\frac{dm}{dt}$ with velocity $\mathbf{v}_{in/out}$ relative to an inertial frame, Newton's second law applied to an instantaneous control volume yields the Meshchersky Equation:

$$\mathbf{F}_{ext} + \mathbf{v}_{rel} \frac{dm}{dt} = m \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}$$

where $\mathbf{v}_{rel} = \mathbf{v}_{exhaust} - \mathbf{v}_{body}$ is the relative velocity of the entering or ejected mass. The term $\mathbf{F}_{thrust} = \mathbf{v}_{rel} \frac{dm}{dt}$ represents the effective thrust force.

For a vertical rocket ejecting mass backward at constant relative speed $u_{ex} = |\mathbf{v}_{rel}|$ in uniform gravity $g$:

$$-mg - u_{ex} \left(-\frac{dm}{dt}\right) = m \frac{dv}{dt} \implies dv = -u_{ex} \frac{dm}{m} - g dt$$

Integrating from initial mass $M_0$ to current mass $m(t)$ yields the celebrated Tsiolkovsky Rocket Equation:

$$v(t) = v_0 + u_{ex} \ln\left(\frac{M_0}{m(t)}\right) - g t$$

4.1.5 Continuous Momentum Transfer: The Falling Chain 3x Theorem

A classic signature problem in JEE Advanced concerns a heavy flexible chain of linear mass density $\lambda = M/L$ held vertically above a scale platform and released from rest. When a length $x$ has collapsed onto the scale, what does the scale read?

Most students calculate the static weight of the accumulated links: $W_{stat} = (\lambda x)g$. But the scale does NOT read $\lambda g x$—it reads three times that value: $3\lambda g x$! Why?

Because falling links strike the pan with velocity $v = \sqrt{2gx}$ and come to an instantaneous stop. To destroy their downward momentum, the scale must deliver a continuous upward impulse. By Newton's Second Law applied to the control volume of the scale platform:

$$F_{scale} = N_{static} + F_{impulsive}$$

The static weight of the coiled chain resting on the scale is simply:

$$N_{static} = (\lambda x)g$$

The rate at which mass enters the scale boundary is $\frac{dm}{dt} = \lambda v$. To bring this influx from speed $v$ to zero requires an impulsive thrust force:

$$F_{impulsive} = v \frac{dm}{dt} = v (\lambda v) = \lambda v^2 = \lambda(2gx) = 2\lambda g x$$

Summing both contributions yields the celebrated 3x Factor Theorem:

$$F_{scale} = \lambda g x + 2\lambda g x = \mathbf{3\lambda g x}$$

The scale reading is triple the weight of the fallen chain! At the instant the last link touches the pan ($x = L$), the scale registers $3Mg$. The moment the entire chain settles into static equilibrium, the impulsive term drops to zero and the reading falls discontinuously to $Mg$.

4.3 10 Forensic NTA/JEE Negative-Marking Trap Alerts

[TRAP ALERT 4.1] The Line of Impact (LOI) Projection Blunder

The Trap: In oblique collisions, setting $e = \frac{v_2 - v_1}{u_1 - u_2}$ using overall velocity magnitudes or laboratory axes rather than the contact normal.

Definitive Rule: Restitution applies strictly and exclusively along the Line of Impact (the common normal at the contact point): $e = \frac{v_{2n} - v_{1n}}{u_{1n} - u_{2n}}$. Velocity components tangential to the contact plane remain completely unaffected: $v_{1t} = u_{1t}$ and $v_{2t} = u_{2t}$.
[TRAP ALERT 4.2] The "COM Divides Area/Mass Equally" Fallacy

The Trap: Assuming that a plane or axis passing through the center of mass bisects the body into two halves of equal mass or area.

Definitive Rule: Center of mass balances first moments of mass ($\int x dm = 0$), NOT zeroth moments ($\int dm$). For a triangle of height $h$, $y_{cm} = h/3$ from the base. A horizontal cut through the COM divides the triangle into areas in the ratio $4:5$, not $1:1$!
[TRAP ALERT 4.3] Man Walking on a Movable Boat / Wedge Shift

The Trap: When a person of mass $m$ walks a distance $L$ from one end of a boat of mass $M$ to the other, students calculate boat displacement as $\Delta x = \frac{m}{M}L$.

Definitive Rule: The distance $L$ is measured relative to the boat. Since net external horizontal force is zero, $\Delta X_{cm} = 0 \implies m(L - \Delta x_{boat}) - M\Delta x_{boat} = 0 \implies \Delta x_{boat} = \mathbf{\frac{m}{M + m}L}$. The denominator is ALWAYS the total system mass $(M+m)$!
[TRAP ALERT 4.4] The Leaking Freight Car Paradox

The Trap: Water leaks vertically through a hole in the bottom of a freight car rolling frictionlessly at speed $v$. Students claim the cart accelerates because its mass decreases.

Definitive Rule: Water leaves with horizontal velocity equal to the cart ($v_{leak, x} = v$). Therefore, $v_{rel, x} = 0$, meaning the leaking water exerts zero horizontal thrust on the cart: $F_{thrust} = v_{rel,x}\frac{dm}{dt} = 0$. The cart's velocity remains strictly constant! (Conversely, if rain falls vertically into the cart, $v_{rel, x} = -v$, so the cart slows down).
[TRAP ALERT 4.5] Momentum Conservation Across Hinges & Pins

The Trap: Conserving linear momentum of a rod-bullet system when the rod is pivoted on a fixed hinge.

Definitive Rule: Fixed hinges exert massive impulsive reaction forces during collision ($\int \mathbf{N}_{hinge} dt \ne \mathbf{0}$). Linear momentum of the rod-bullet system is NOT conserved. You MUST conserve angular momentum about the hinge axis, where external reaction torque is identically zero ($\boldsymbol{\tau}_{hinge} = \mathbf{0}$).
[TRAP ALERT 4.6] Completely Inelastic Collision Does NOT Mean Zero Final Kinetic Energy

The Trap: Equating $e = 0$ (perfectly inelastic) to $\Delta K = K_{initial}$, leaving zero final kinetic energy.

Definitive Rule: In an isolated system, linear momentum must be conserved. Therefore, $K_{final} = \frac{P_{total}^2}{2(m_1 + m_2)} > 0$ unless initial total momentum was already zero in the lab frame! Exactly the relative kinetic energy $K_{rel} = \frac{1}{2}\mu u_{rel}^2$ is dissipated; $K_{cm} = \frac{P^2}{2M_{tot}}$ is indestructible.
[TRAP ALERT 4.7] Impulsive Friction Thresholding During Impact

The Trap: Applying Coulomb's static friction condition $f \le \mu N$ using normal force $N = mg$ during collision impacts.

Definitive Rule: During impact, normal reaction is impulsive: $N_{imp} \approx \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} \gg mg$. The friction impulse is $J_f = \int f dt \le \mu \int N dt = \mu J_N$. Gravitational contribution is utterly negligible during the microsecond contact interval.
[TRAP ALERT 4.8] Negative Sign in Meshchersky Rocket Thrust

The Trap: Writing $m \frac{dv}{dt} = v_{rel} \frac{dm}{dt}$ and ending up with deceleration because $\frac{dm}{dt} < 0$.

Definitive Rule: Ejected mass travels backward: $\mathbf{v}_{rel} = -u_{ex} \hat{\mathbf{i}}$. Rate of mass change is negative: $\frac{dm}{dt} = -R_{burn}$. Thrust force is $\mathbf{F}_{thrust} = (-u_{ex}\hat{\mathbf{i}})(-R_{burn}) = +u_{ex} R_{burn} \hat{\mathbf{i}}$ (forward acceleration). Always verify physical thrust direction from first principles!
[TRAP ALERT 4.9] Maximum Compression of a Spring Between Moving Masses

The Trap: Assuming maximum spring compression between two free masses on a smooth floor occurs when the first mass comes to rest in the lab frame ($v_1 = 0$).

Definitive Rule: Maximum spring compression occurs when the spring stops compressing, which means relative velocity is zero: $\mathbf{v}_1 = \mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{V}_{cm}$. The energy stored in the spring is $U_{s,\max} = \frac{1}{2} k x_{\max}^2 = \frac{1}{2}\mu u_{rel}^2$.
[TRAP ALERT 4.10] Center of Mass Velocity with Internal Explosions

The Trap: A projectile explodes mid-air into fragments. Students attempt to recalculate the trajectory of the COM using the explosive fragment speeds.

Definitive Rule: Explosive internal forces sum to zero ($\sum \mathbf{F}_{int} = \mathbf{0}$). Gravity is the sole external force acting on the system. Therefore, the center of mass of the fragments continues along the original parabolic trajectory until the first fragment strikes the ground!

[WORKED JEE ADVANCED PYQ] JEE Advanced 2018 • Paper 1 Difficulty: Hard • Oblique Rebound on Incline
A small ball is dropped vertically from height $h = 5\text{ m}$ onto a smooth inclined plane of angle $\theta = 30^\circ$ fixed to the ground. The coefficient of restitution between the ball and the plane is $e = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$. Find the distance along the incline between the first impact point and the second impact point. Take $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$.
First-Principles Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Impact Velocity & Axis Setup:

Speed just before impact: $u = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 5} = 10\text{ m/s}$ directed vertically downward.

Choose coordinate axes aligned with the incline:

  • $x$-axis: Along the incline downwards.
  • $y$-axis: Perpendicular to the incline upwards (Line of Impact).

Components of approach velocity just before impact:

$$u_x = u \sin\theta = 10 \sin 30^\circ = 5\text{ m/s}$$ $$u_y = -u \cos\theta = -10 \cos 30^\circ = -5\sqrt{3}\text{ m/s}$$

Components of acceleration due to gravity:

$$g_x = g \sin\theta = 10 \sin 30^\circ = 5\text{ m/s}^2$$ $$g_y = -g \cos\theta = -10 \cos 30^\circ = -5\sqrt{3}\text{ m/s}^2$$

Step 2: Velocity Components Immediately After Impact:

Since the incline is smooth, parallel impulse is zero: $v_{1x} = u_x = \mathbf{5\text{ m/s}}$.

Along the normal (LOI), restitution applies against a fixed mass ($M \to \infty$):

$$v_{1y} = e |u_y| = \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)(5\sqrt{3}) = \mathbf{5\text{ m/s}}$$

Step 3: Time of Flight to Second Impact ($y = 0$):

$$y(T) = v_{1y} T - \frac{1}{2} (g\cos\theta) T^2 = 0 \implies T = \frac{2 v_{1y}}{g \cos\theta} = \frac{2(5)}{5\sqrt{3}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\text{ seconds}$$

Step 4: Distance Traversed Along Incline:

$$S_x = v_{1x} T + \frac{1}{2} g_x T^2 = 5\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\right) + \frac{1}{2}(5)\left(\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}\right)^2 = \frac{10}{\sqrt{3}} + \frac{5}{2}\left(\frac{4}{3}\right) = \frac{10}{\sqrt{3}} + \frac{10}{3}\text{ m}$$ $$S_x = \mathbf{\frac{10(\sqrt{3} + 1)}{3}\text{ m}} \approx 9.11\text{ meters}$$
[WORKED JEE ADVANCED PYQ] JEE Advanced 2015 • Paper 2 Difficulty: Hard • Spring-Coupled Momentum & Maximum Elongation
Two blocks of masses $m_1 = 2\text{ kg}$ and $m_2 = 3\text{ kg}$ are connected by a light spring of force constant $k = 300\text{ N/m}$ on a smooth horizontal floor. Initially, the system is at rest. Block $m_1$ is given an instantaneous horizontal impulse $J = 12\text{ N}\cdot\text{s}$ away from $m_2$. Determine (a) the velocity of the center of mass $V_{cm}$, (b) the maximum elongation $x_{\max}$ of the spring, and (c) the minimum speed attained by block $m_1$ during subsequent motion.
First-Principles Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Center of Mass Kinematics:

Velocity of $m_1$ immediately after impulse: $u_1 = \frac{J}{m_1} = \frac{12}{2} = 6\text{ m/s}$, while $u_2 = 0$.

$$V_{cm} = \frac{m_1 u_1 + m_2 u_2}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{2(6) + 3(0)}{2 + 3} = \frac{12}{5} = \mathbf{2.4\text{ m/s}}$$

Since no external horizontal force acts after impulse, $V_{cm}$ remains invariant throughout the motion.

Step 2: Maximum Spring Elongation:

Calculate reduced mass: $\mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2} = \frac{2 \times 3}{2 + 3} = \frac{6}{5} = 1.2\text{ kg}$.

Initial relative velocity: $u_{rel} = u_1 - u_2 = 6 - 0 = 6\text{ m/s}$.

At maximum elongation, relative velocity is zero ($v_{rel} = 0$). By Koenig's theorem, all relative kinetic energy is converted into elastic potential energy:

$$\frac{1}{2} k x_{\max}^2 = \frac{1}{2} \mu u_{rel}^2 \implies x_{\max} = u_{rel}\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{k}} = 6\sqrt{\frac{1.2}{300}} = 6\sqrt{\frac{1}{250}} = \frac{6}{5\sqrt{10}} = \mathbf{\frac{6\sqrt{10}}{50}\text{ m}} \approx 37.9\text{ cm}$$

In the C-frame, block $m_1$ oscillates with velocity amplitude: $v_{1}^* = \frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2} u_{rel} = \frac{3}{5}(6) = 3.6\text{ m/s}$.

Velocity in lab frame: $v_1(t) = V_{cm} + v_1^*(t) = 2.4 + 3.6 \cos(\omega t)$.

When $\cos(\omega t) = -1$:

$$v_{1, \min} = 2.4 - 3.6 = \mathbf{-1.2\text{ m/s}}$$

The minimum speed (magnitude) is $|v_1| = 0\text{ m/s}$ (since velocity crosses zero as it transitions from $+6$ to $-1.2$). The minimum algebraic velocity is $-1.2\text{ m/s}$.

[WORKED JEE ADVANCED PYQ] JEE Advanced 2020 • Paper 1 Difficulty: Hard • Continuous Mass Deposition on Rolling Cart
An open rectangular cart of mass $M_0 = 50\text{ kg}$ is coasting horizontally on a frictionless track with speed $v_0 = 10\text{ m/s}$. Rain begins to fall vertically downward into the open top of the cart at a constant mass deposition rate $\alpha = \frac{dm}{dt} = 2\text{ kg/s}$. Find (a) the velocity of the cart after $t = 25\text{ seconds}$, and (b) the horizontal external force required to keep the cart moving at its initial constant speed $v_0$.
First-Principles Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Velocity Under Zero Horizontal External Force:

Rain falls vertically, so it possesses zero initial horizontal momentum ($p_{rain, x} = 0$).

Because there is no external horizontal force on the cart-water system, total horizontal momentum is conserved:

$$P_x(t) = [M_0 + m_{water}(t)] v(t) = M_0 v_0 = \text{constant}$$ $$[M_0 + \alpha t] v(t) = M_0 v_0 \implies v(t) = \frac{M_0 v_0}{M_0 + \alpha t}$$

At $t = 25\text{ s}$:

$$v(25) = \frac{50 \times 10}{50 + 2(25)} = \frac{500}{100} = \mathbf{5.0\text{ m/s}}$$

Step 2: Force Required for Constant Velocity $v_0$:

From Meshchersky's equation, the relative horizontal velocity of incoming rain is $v_{rel, x} = 0 - v_0 = -v_0$.

$$F_{ext} + v_{rel, x} \frac{dm}{dt} = m(t) \frac{dv}{dt}$$

To maintain constant speed $v = v_0$, acceleration must be zero ($\frac{dv}{dt} = 0$):

$$F_{ext} + (-v_0) \alpha = 0 \implies F_{ext} = \alpha v_0 = (2\text{ kg/s})(10\text{ m/s}) = \mathbf{20\text{ Newtons}}$$

Physical Interpretation: The motor must deliver $20\text{ N}$ forward to accelerate newly captured rain droplets from $0$ up to $v_0$ horizontally.

View Complete Analytical Solution

Let linear mass density be $\lambda = M/L = 3/2 = 1.5\text{ kg/m}$.

The total normal force measured by the scale consists of two distinct contributions:

$$N_{total} = \lambda g x + 2\lambda g x = \mathbf{3\lambda g x}$$

[MENTOR STRATEGY NOTE] Why Rotational Dynamics Makes or Breaks Top Ranks

Listen to me very carefully: Rotational mechanics accounts for 14–16% of JEE Advanced Physics. It is historically the single highest-yield, highest-negative-marking chapter in the entire syllabus. Once you understand how to pick your reference axis—especially the Center of Mass and the Instantaneous Axis of Rotation (IAOR)—the problems solve themselves in 4 lines. Let's master the mechanics.

5.1 First-Principles Foundations: Rigid Body Dynamics & Kinematics

5.1.1 Moment of Inertia, Steiner's Theorem & The 2D Lamina Restriction

Think of Moment of Inertia $I$ as "rotational stubbornness." In straight-line motion, mass $m$ measures how stubborn an object is against changing speed ($F = ma$). In rotation, Moment of Inertia does the exact same job for spinning ($\tau = I\alpha$). But while mass is fixed, rotational stubbornness depends heavily on how far the mass sits from the spin axis:

$$I = \int r_\perp^2 dm$$

where $r_\perp$ is the perpendicular distance from each mass element $dm$ to the chosen axis of rotation.

[KEY PRINCIPLE] Foundational Axis Theorems & Their Strict Domain of Validity

1. Parallel Axis Theorem (Steiner's Theorem):

$$I_{axis} = I_{cm} + M d_\perp^2$$

Strict Condition: One of the two parallel axes MUST pass through the Center of Mass (COM). The distance $d_\perp$ is the perpendicular distance between the two parallel axes. It is a fatal blunder to apply this theorem directly between two arbitrary non-COM axes!

2. Perpendicular Axis Theorem:

$$I_z = I_x + I_y$$

Strict Condition: Valid ONLY for 2D Planar Laminas lying completely in the $xy$-plane ($z \equiv 0$). The $z$-axis must be perpendicular to the lamina and pass through the intersection point of the mutually perpendicular $x$ and $y$ axes. Applying this theorem to a 3D sphere, cylinder, or cube is a gross conceptual error!

Canonical Continuous Body Moments of Inertia (JEE Advanced Standard):

  • Uniform Thin Rod ($M, L$): $I_{cm,\perp} = \frac{1}{12}ML^2$; End axis $I_{end,\perp} = \frac{1}{3}ML^2$; Inclined at angle $\theta$: $I = \frac{1}{12}ML^2\sin^2\theta$.
  • Uniform Circular Ring ($M, R$): Central transverse axis $I_z = MR^2$; Diameter axis $I_d = \frac{1}{2}MR^2$; Tangent in-plane $I_{tan,\parallel} = \frac{3}{2}MR^2$; Tangent perpendicular $I_{tan,\perp} = 2MR^2$.
  • Uniform Circular Disc ($M, R$): Central transverse axis $I_z = \frac{1}{2}MR^2$; Diameter axis $I_d = \frac{1}{4}MR^2$; Tangent in-plane $I_{tan,\parallel} = \frac{5}{4}MR^2$; Tangent perpendicular $I_{tan,\perp} = \frac{3}{2}MR^2$.
  • Uniform Thin Spherical Shell ($M, R$): Diameter axis $I_d = \mathbf{\frac{2}{3}MR^2}$; Tangent axis $I_{tan} = \frac{5}{3}MR^2$.
  • Uniform Solid Sphere ($M, R$): Diameter axis $I_d = \mathbf{\frac{2}{5}MR^2}$; Tangent axis $I_{tan} = \frac{7}{5}MR^2$.
  • Uniform Solid Cylinder ($M, R, L$): Longitudinal axis $I_z = \frac{1}{2}MR^2$; Central transverse axis $I_{trans, cm} = \frac{1}{4}MR^2 + \frac{1}{12}ML^2$.

5.1.2 The Generalized Torque Equation About Arbitrary Reference Points

Let $O$ be an arbitrary reference point that may itself accelerate with acceleration $\mathbf{a}_O$ relative to an inertial frame. The fundamental angular momentum equation is:

$$\boldsymbol{\tau}_O = \frac{d\mathbf{L}_O}{dt} + \mathbf{v}_O \times \mathbf{P}$$

where $\mathbf{P} = M\mathbf{v}_{cm}$ is the total linear momentum. The simple formula $\boldsymbol{\tau}_O = I_O \boldsymbol{\alpha}$ holds strictly if and only if one of the following three conditions is met:

  1. The reference point $O$ is stationary in an inertial frame ($\mathbf{v}_O = \mathbf{0}, \mathbf{a}_O = \mathbf{0}$).
  2. The reference point $O$ is the Center of Mass ($O \equiv C$): Here $\mathbf{r}_{cm/O} = \mathbf{0}$, meaning pseudo-forces exert identically zero torque: $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{cm} = I_{cm}\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ unconditionally in any reference frame!
  3. The acceleration of point $O$ is directed along the line connecting $O$ and the Center of Mass ($\mathbf{a}_O \parallel \mathbf{r}_{cm/O}$): The cross product $\mathbf{r}_{cm/O} \times (-M\mathbf{a}_O) = \mathbf{0}$.

If torque is evaluated about an arbitrary accelerating point $P$ not satisfying these conditions, one MUST include the torque of the pseudo-force acting at the center of mass:

$$\boldsymbol{\tau}_{P, net} = \boldsymbol{\tau}_{P, real} + \mathbf{r}_{cm/P} \times (-M \mathbf{a}_P) = I_P \boldsymbol{\alpha}$$

5.1.3 Pure Rolling Dynamics on Horizontal & Inclined Planes

Pure rolling is the superposition of translational motion of the center of mass and rotation about the center of mass, subject to the non-slip kinematic constraint at the instantaneous contact point $C$:

$$\mathbf{v}_C = \mathbf{v}_{cm} + \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{0} \implies v_{cm} = \omega R$$ $$\mathbf{a}_{C, tangential} = \mathbf{a}_{cm} - \boldsymbol{\alpha} R = \mathbf{0} \implies a_{cm} = \alpha R$$

Crucial Acceleration Fact: While the tangential acceleration of the contact point is zero, its radial/centripetal acceleration is non-zero: $\mathbf{a}_C = \omega^2 R \hat{\mathbf{n}}$ directed toward the wheel center. The contact point is executing a cycloid path!

Pure Rolling Down an Inclined Plane of Angle $\theta$:

Writing Newton's second law along the incline and torque about the Center of Mass:

$$Mg\sin\theta - f_s = M a_{cm}$$ $$\tau_{cm} = f_s R = I_{cm} \alpha = I_{cm} \left(\frac{a_{cm}}{R}\right) \implies f_s = \frac{I_{cm}}{R^2} a_{cm}$$

Substituting $f_s$ into the linear equation yields the universal rolling acceleration equation:

$$a_{cm} = \frac{g \sin\theta}{1 + \frac{I_{cm}}{MR^2}} = \frac{g \sin\theta}{1 + \frac{k^2}{R^2}}$$

where $k$ is the radius of gyration. The required static friction force to prevent slipping is:

$$f_s = \frac{Mg\sin\theta}{1 + \frac{MR^2}{I_{cm}}}$$

To ensure pure rolling without slipping, $f_s \le \mu_s N = \mu_s Mg\cos\theta$. This establishes the critical incline angle threshold:

$$\tan\theta_c = \mu_s \left(1 + \frac{MR^2}{I_{cm}}\right)$$

5.1.4 Toppling vs. Sliding Mechanics

Consider a rectangular block of mass $M$, width $b$, and height $h$ resting on a horizontal plane with static friction coefficient $\mu_s$. A horizontal force $F$ is applied at height $y$ above the base.

  • Normal Force Shift: As $F$ increases, the normal force shifts forward by distance $x_N$ from the center to counteract the overturning torque $F y$. Torque balance about COM gives: $$N x_N = F\left(y - \frac{h}{2}\right) + f_s \left(\frac{h}{2}\right) = F y \implies x_N = \frac{F y}{Mg}$$
  • Toppling Condition: The block begins to topple when the normal force shifts all the way to the forward edge: $x_{N,\max} = \frac{b}{2}$. The critical toppling force is: $$F_{topple} = \frac{b}{2y} Mg$$
  • Sliding Condition: The block begins to slide when $F$ overcomes maximum static friction: $$F_{slide} = \mu_s Mg$$
  • Universal Criterion:
    • If $F_{slide} < F_{topple} \iff \mu_s < \frac{b}{2y}$: The block slides before toppling.
    • If $F_{topple} < F_{slide} \iff \mu_s > \frac{b}{2y}$: The block topples before sliding.

5.1.5 The Instantaneous Center of Rotation (IAOR) Velocity Vector Field

For any rigid body undergoing planar general motion, there exists at every instant a unique point in space (which may lie inside or outside the body) that has dead zero velocity: $\mathbf{v}_{IAOR} = \mathbf{0}$. This point is the Instantaneous Axis of Rotation (IAOR).

The Beauty of the IAOR:

At that instant, the complex combined motion of translation plus rotation reduces purely to pure rotation about the IAOR with angular velocity $\omega$! The velocity of any particle $P$ in the body is simply:

$$\mathbf{v}_P = \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{r}_{P/IAOR} \implies v_P = \omega r_P$$

and the direction of $\mathbf{v}_P$ is perpendicular to the line joining $P$ to the IAOR. Furthermore, kinetic energy simplifies to a single term:

$$K = \frac{1}{2} I_{IAOR} \omega^2$$

By Steiner's parallel axis theorem, $I_{IAOR} = I_{cm} + M R^2$, which immediately yields $\frac{1}{2}(I_{cm} + MR^2)\omega^2 = \frac{1}{2}I_{cm}\omega^2 + \frac{1}{2}Mv_{cm}^2$. The two descriptions are mathematically and physically identical!

[TRAP ALERT 5.2] Perpendicular Axis Theorem on a Solid Cylinder or Sphere

The Trap: Finding moment of inertia of a solid cylinder of radius $R$ about diameter by claiming $I_z = I_x + I_y \implies I_d = \frac{1}{2}I_z = \frac{1}{4}MR^2$.

Definitive Rule: The perpendicular axis theorem is valid strictly for 2D flat sheets! A cylinder has length $L$, so its transverse moment of inertia is $I_{\text{trans}} = \frac{1}{4}MR^2 + \frac{1}{12}ML^2$. Setting $L = 0$ is valid only for an infinitesimally thin disc.
[TRAP ALERT 5.3] Friction Direction in Pure Rolling

The Trap: Assuming static friction on a rolling wheel always acts backward to oppose translational motion.

Definitive Rule: Friction acts to prevent relative slipping at the contact point. If a wheel is driven by an axle torque $\tau$ (like the rear wheel of an accelerating car), static friction acts FORWARD to propel the vehicle! On an unpowered front wheel, static friction acts backward to provide rotational angular acceleration.
[TRAP ALERT 5.4] Writing $\tau = I\alpha$ About an Accelerating Point

The Trap: Writing $\tau_P = I_P \alpha$ about the contact point of an accelerating rolling body when the contact surface is itself accelerating (e.g., rolling inside an accelerating elevator or truck).

Definitive Rule: Point $P$ accelerates, so you must include the pseudo-torque: $\boldsymbol{\tau}_{P, real} + \mathbf{r}_{cm/P} \times (-M\mathbf{a}_P) = I_P \boldsymbol{\alpha}$. Alternatively and much more reliably: Always take torque about the Center of Mass (COM), where pseudo-torque is identically zero regardless of any reference frame acceleration!
[TRAP ALERT 5.5] Released Horizontal Rod Tip Acceleration $> g$

The Trap: A uniform rod of length $L$ is pivoted horizontally at one end and released from rest. Students assume no part of the rod can accelerate faster than $g$.

Definitive Rule: Torque about the hinge: $\tau = Mg\left(\frac{L}{2}\right) = I_{hinge} \alpha = \left(\frac{1}{3}ML^2\right)\alpha \implies \alpha = \frac{3g}{2L}$. The tangential acceleration of the free tip is $a_{tip} = \alpha L = \mathbf{\frac{3}{2}g = 1.5g > g}$! An object resting on the tip will lose contact immediately and undergo free fall ($a = g$).
[TRAP ALERT 5.6] Misapplying Steiner's Theorem Between Non-COM Axes

The Trap: Given moment of inertia about one end of a rod $I_{end} = \frac{1}{3}ML^2$, finding $I$ about an axis at distance $d = L/4$ from that end by adding $M(L/4)^2$.

Definitive Rule: Steiner's theorem works ONLY relative to the COM: $I_{new} = I_{cm} + M d_{cm}^2 = \frac{1}{12}ML^2 + M(L/4)^2 = \frac{1}{12}ML^2 + \frac{1}{16}ML^2 = \frac{7}{48}ML^2$. You cannot shift directly between two non-COM axes!
[TRAP ALERT 5.7] Kinetic Energy of Rolling without Slipping

The Trap: Calculating total kinetic energy of a rolling body as either purely $\frac{1}{2}M v_{cm}^2$ or purely $\frac{1}{2}I_{cm}\omega^2$.

Definitive Rule: By Koenig's theorem: $K_{total} = \frac{1}{2}M v_{cm}^2 + \frac{1}{2}I_{cm}\omega^2 = \frac{1}{2}M v_{cm}^2\left(1 + \frac{k^2}{R^2}\right)$. Alternatively, using the Instantaneous Axis of Rotation (IAOR): $K_{total} = \frac{1}{2}I_{IAOR}\omega^2$. Both yield identical results.
[TRAP ALERT 5.8] Toppling on Incline vs. Sliding Competition

The Trap: Assuming a cube on an incline always topples when the tilt reaches $\theta = 45^\circ$.

Definitive Rule: For a cube of side $a$, toppling occurs when $\tan\theta = \frac{a/2}{a/2} = 1 \implies \theta = 45^\circ$. HOWEVER, if the coefficient of friction satisfies $\mu < 1$, the cube will start sliding at $\theta = \arctan(\mu) < 45^\circ$ before toppling can ever occur! Always compare $\theta_{slide} = \arctan\mu$ with $\theta_{topple} = \arctan(b/h)$.
[TRAP ALERT 5.9] Conservation of Angular Momentum Across Hinges

The Trap: Conserving angular momentum about the Center of Mass when a projectile strikes a hinged rod.

Definitive Rule: The hinge exerts an unknown external impulsive reaction force $\mathbf{J}_{hinge}$. This reaction creates a non-zero torque about the Center of Mass ($\boldsymbol{\tau}_{cm} \ne \mathbf{0}$). You MUST conserve angular momentum about the hinge pivot, where the lever arm of the hinge reaction is identically zero!
[TRAP ALERT 5.10] Rolling with Slipping Transition Time

The Trap: Projecting a spinning bowling ball onto a rough floor with $\omega_0$ and $v_0 = 0$. Students assume static friction acts immediately.

Definitive Rule: Slipping occurs initially, so friction is kinetic: $f_k = \mu_k Mg$ (constant). Acceleration is $a_{cm} = \mu_k g$ forward, and angular deceleration is $\alpha = \frac{f_k R}{I_{cm}} = \frac{\mu_k Mg R}{I_{cm}}$ backward. Pure rolling begins only when $v(t) = \omega(t) R$. From that instant onward, friction drops discontinuously to zero on a level floor!

[WORKED JEE ADVANCED PYQ] JEE Advanced 2016 • Paper 1 Difficulty: Hard • Pure Rolling on Incline Transition
A uniform disc of mass $M = 2\text{ kg}$ and radius $R = 0.5\text{ m}$ is rolling without slipping on a horizontal surface with linear speed $v_0 = 6\text{ m/s}$. It smoothly ascends a rough incline of inclination angle $\theta = 30^\circ$. The coefficient of static friction between the disc and the incline is $\mu_s = 0.4$. (a) Determine whether the disc rolls without slipping up the incline. (b) Find the maximum vertical height $h_{\max}$ reached by the disc. Take $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$.
First-Principles Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Check Condition for Pure Rolling on Incline:

For a uniform disc, $I_{cm} = \frac{1}{2}MR^2$, so the radius of gyration ratio is $\frac{k^2}{R^2} = \frac{1}{2}$.

The minimum coefficient of static friction required to prevent slipping on an incline is:

$$\mu_{\min} = \frac{\tan\theta}{1 + \frac{MR^2}{I_{cm}}} = \frac{\tan 30^\circ}{1 + 2} = \frac{1}{3\sqrt{3}} \approx 0.192$$

Since the given friction coefficient $\mu_s = 0.4 > 0.192$, the disc rolls purely without slipping during the entire ascent!

Step 2: Conservation of Total Mechanical Energy:

In pure rolling, static friction does zero net mechanical work ($W_{f_s} = 0$). Therefore, total mechanical energy is conserved between the floor and maximum height:

$$E_i = \frac{1}{2}M v_0^2 + \frac{1}{2}I_{cm}\omega_0^2 = \frac{1}{2}M v_0^2 + \frac{1}{4}M v_0^2 = \frac{3}{4}M v_0^2$$ $$E_f = M g h_{\max}$$

Equating initial and final energy:

$$M g h_{\max} = \frac{3}{4} M v_0^2 \implies h_{\max} = \frac{3 v_0^2}{4 g}$$

Substituting $v_0 = 6\text{ m/s}$ and $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$:

$$h_{\max} = \frac{3(36)}{40} = \mathbf{2.70\text{ meters}}$$

Distance traveled along incline: $s = \frac{h_{\max}}{\sin 30^\circ} = \frac{2.7}{0.5} = \mathbf{5.4\text{ meters}}$.

[WORKED JEE ADVANCED PYQ] JEE Advanced 2019 • Paper 1 Difficulty: Hard • Pivoted Rod Hinge Reaction Dynamics
A uniform thin rod of mass $M = 1.5\text{ kg}$ and length $L = 1.0\text{ m}$ is pivoted about a frictionless horizontal axis passing through one end. The rod is released from rest from a horizontal position. Find the horizontal and vertical components of the hinge reaction force on the rod at the instant it has rotated through angle $\theta = 60^\circ$ from the initial horizontal. Take $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$.
First-Principles Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Angular Velocity from Energy Conservation:

The Center of Mass is at distance $d = L/2$ from the hinge. When the rod drops by $\theta = 60^\circ$, COM falls by $\Delta h = \frac{L}{2}\sin 60^\circ = \frac{L\sqrt{3}}{4}$.

$$\Delta U = Mg\left(\frac{L\sqrt{3}}{4}\right) = \frac{1}{2} I_{hinge} \omega^2 = \frac{1}{6}ML^2 \omega^2$$ $$\omega^2 = \frac{3\sqrt{3}g}{2L}$$

Step 2: Angular Acceleration from Torque:

Torque about the hinge at angle $\theta$ below horizontal:

$$\tau_{hinge} = Mg\left(\frac{L}{2}\cos\theta\right) = \left(\frac{1}{3}ML^2\right)\alpha$$ $$\alpha = \frac{3g\cos\theta}{2L}$$

At $\theta = 60^\circ$: $\alpha = \frac{3g(1/2)}{2L} = \frac{3g}{4L}$.

Step 3: Accelerations of the Center of Mass:

Tangential acceleration of COM: $a_t = \alpha \left(\frac{L}{2}\right) = \frac{3g}{8}$.

Radial centripetal acceleration toward hinge: $a_r = \omega^2 \left(\frac{L}{2}\right) = \frac{3\sqrt{3}g}{4}$.

Step 4: Hinge Reaction Components:

Resolving accelerations horizontally ($x$) and vertically ($y$):

$$a_x = a_r \cos 60^\circ - a_t \sin 60^\circ = \frac{3\sqrt{3}g}{8} - \frac{3\sqrt{3}g}{16} = \frac{3\sqrt{3}g}{16}$$ $$N_x = M a_x = (1.5)\left(\frac{3\sqrt{3}(10)}{16}\right) \approx \mathbf{4.87\text{ N}}$$ $$a_y = a_r \sin 60^\circ + a_t \cos 60^\circ = \frac{9g}{8} + \frac{3g}{16} = \frac{21g}{16}$$

Vertical equation of motion: $Mg - N_y = M a_y \implies N_y = M(g - a_y) = -\frac{5Mg}{16}$

$$N_y = -\frac{5(1.5)(10)}{16} = \mathbf{-4.69\text{ N}} \quad (\text{hinge pulls downward!})$$
[WORKED JEE ADVANCED PYQ] JEE Advanced 2021 • Paper 2 Difficulty: Hard • Horizontal Force Applied to Rolling Cylinder
A solid uniform cylinder of mass $M = 4\text{ kg}$ and radius $R = 0.2\text{ m}$ rests on a rough horizontal floor with static friction coefficient $\mu_s = 0.3$. A horizontal pulling force $F = 18\text{ N}$ is applied to a string wound around the top of the cylinder (at height $2R$ above the ground). Determine (a) the acceleration of the center of mass $a_{cm}$, (b) the magnitude and direction of the static friction force $f_s$, and (c) the maximum force $F_{\max}$ for which pure rolling without slipping is maintained.
First-Principles Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Equations of Motion Assuming Pure Rolling:

Let friction $f_s$ act forward in the direction of $F$ (we will let the sign verify direction):

$$F + f_s = M a_{cm}$$

Taking torque about the Center of Mass (clockwise positive):

$$\tau_{cm} = F R - f_s R = I_{cm} \alpha = \left(\frac{1}{2}MR^2\right)\left(\frac{a_{cm}}{R}\right) = \frac{1}{2}MR a_{cm}$$ $$F - f_s = \frac{1}{2} M a_{cm}$$

Step 2: Solve Acceleration and Friction:

Adding the two equations:

$$2F = \frac{3}{2} M a_{cm} \implies a_{cm} = \frac{4F}{3M}$$ $$a_{cm} = \frac{4(18)}{3(4)} = \frac{72}{12} = \mathbf{6.0\text{ m/s}^2}$$

Subtracting the torque equation from the force equation:

$$2f_s = \frac{1}{2} M a_{cm} \implies f_s = \frac{1}{4} M a_{cm} = \frac{1}{4} M \left(\frac{4F}{3M}\right) = \frac{F}{3}$$ $$f_s = \frac{18}{3} = \mathbf{+6.0\text{ N}} \quad (\text{acts FORWARD in direction of } F!)$$

Step 3: Threshold for Slipping ($f_s \le \mu_s N$):

Normal force: $N = Mg = 4(10) = 40\text{ N}$. Maximum static friction: $f_{s,\max} = \mu_s N = 0.3(40) = 12\text{ N}$.

$$f_s = \frac{F}{3} \le 12\text{ N} \implies F_{\max} = 3(12) = \mathbf{36\text{ Newtons}}$$

Since $F = 18\text{ N} < 36\text{ N}$, the assumption of pure rolling is fully valid!

Gravitation & Oscillatory Motion (SHM)

[MENTOR STRATEGY NOTE] Where Gravitation Meets Harmonics

Listen up, aspirants: Gravitation and Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM) are intimately linked by nature's inverse-square and linear force fields. In JEE Advanced, the examiners almost never ask a simple "plug $T = 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}$" question. They test physical disc pendulums pivoted at their rim, off-center Earth tunnels where harmonic restoring forces survive along oblique chords, orbital transfers via instantaneous rocket impulses (Vis-Viva), and two-block spring oscillators where reduced mass is mandatory.

The Golden Truth: Potential is an integral; field is a derivative. Potential at the center of the Earth is $-1.5 GM/R$, NOT zero. And a satellite fired with reverse brakes actually speeds up as it drops to a lower orbit. Let's master the mathematical mechanics so you dominate Paper 1 and Paper 2.

6.1 First-Principles Foundations: Gravitational Fields & Harmonics

6.1.1 Continuous Body Gravitational Potentials & Fields

Think of gravitational potential $V$ like altitude on a landscape. Gravitational field $\mathbf{E}$ is simply how steep that slope is, pulling naturally downhill toward lower potential: $\mathbf{E} = -\frac{dV}{dr}\hat{\mathbf{r}}$. Because gravity only pulls things together, we agree by universal convention to set our zero-reference point infinitely far away: $V(\infty) = 0$. Anywhere closer to a mass, you are sitting down inside a gravity well, which is why gravitational potential is always negative.

  • Uniform Thin Spherical Shell of Mass $M$ and Radius $R$:

    Inside a hollow spherical shell, the gravitational pull from all sides cancels out completely. The field is zero everywhere inside, so the potential is flat and constant, equal to its value on the surface:

    $$\text{Inside } (r < R): \quad E(r) = 0, \quad V(r) = -\frac{GM}{R} = \text{constant}$$

    Outside the shell, it pulls exactly as if all its mass were concentrated at the center point:

    $$\text{Outside } (r \ge R): \quad E(r) = \frac{GM}{r^2}, \quad V(r) = -\frac{GM}{r}$$
  • Uniform Solid Sphere of Mass $M$ and Radius $R$ (Volume density $\rho = \frac{3M}{4\pi R^3}$):

    As you dig deeper into a uniform solid planet, only the sphere of mass beneath your feet exerts net gravity:

    $$\text{Inside } (r \le R): \quad E(r) = \frac{G M(r)}{r^2} = \mathbf{\frac{GM r}{R^3}}$$

    Integrating the field inward from the surface gives the potential at any internal radius $r$:

    $$V(r) = -\frac{GM}{2R^3}\left(3R^2 - r^2\right)$$

    At the exact center ($r = 0$), the field is zero because mass pulls equally from all directions, but potential reaches its deepest negative pit:

    $$V(0) = -\frac{3}{2}\frac{GM}{R} = \mathbf{-1.5 \frac{GM}{R}}$$
  • Gravitational Self-Energy ($U_{self}$):

    This is simply the total work done by gravity in pulling scattered cosmic dust together from infinity to assemble the object:

    $$\text{Thin Spherical Shell: } U_{self} = \mathbf{-\frac{GM^2}{2R}}$$ $$\text{Solid Sphere: } U_{self} = \mathbf{-\frac{3}{5}\frac{GM^2}{R}}$$

6.1.2 Orbital Mechanics & The Vis-Viva Energy Equation

Every closed gravitational orbit is an ellipse with the central star sitting at one focus (Kepler's First Law). Because gravity pulls directly toward the star, it exerts zero torque ($\boldsymbol{\tau} = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}_g = \mathbf{0}$). With zero torque, angular momentum $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r}\times m\mathbf{v}$ is strictly conserved throughout the orbit. This constant angular momentum is the physical reason planets sweep out equal areas in equal times (Kepler's Second Law: $\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{L}{2m}$).

The Vis-Viva Equation is simply conservation of energy in its most practical form. Instead of recalculating kinetic and potential energy each time, it directly links speed $v$, distance from the star $r$, and the size of the orbit $a$ in one clean formula:

$$v^2 = GM\left(\frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a}\right)$$
  • Circular Orbit ($a = r$): Speed is constant everywhere: $v_{circ} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$, with orbital period $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{GM}}$.
  • Parabolic Escape Trajectory ($a \to \infty$): The exact speed needed to escape the star's gravity completely: $v_{esc} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}} = \sqrt{2} v_{circ}$.
  • Kepler's Third Law: The square of orbital period scales with the cube of the semi-major axis: $$T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{GM} a^3$$

6.1.3 Advanced Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM) Dynamics

Simple Harmonic Motion is what happens whenever an object feels a restoring force proportional to how far it is displaced: $F = -kx$. The further you push it, the harder it pushes back. This yields the universal oscillation equation:

$$\ddot{x} + \omega^2 x = 0 \implies x(t) = A\sin(\omega t + \phi)$$

Speed and acceleration at any position $x$ follow naturally:

$$v(x) = \pm \omega \sqrt{A^2 - x^2}, \quad a(x) = -\omega^2 x$$

Two-Block Oscillator with Spring (Reduced Mass):

When two free blocks of mass $m_1$ and $m_2$ oscillate on a frictionless floor connected by a spring of stiffness $k$, they oscillate in opposition about their stationary common Center of Mass. The system behaves identically to a single block of effective "reduced mass" $\mu$ attached to a fixed wall:

$$T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{k}} \quad \text{where } \mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}$$

Heavy Spring Mass Correction:

If the spring itself has mass $m_s$, coils near the wall barely move while coils near the load move at full speed. Integrating the kinetic energy of all moving coils shows that the spring effectively adds exactly one-third of its mass to the oscillating load:

$$m_{eff} = M + \frac{1}{3}m_s \implies T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{M + \frac{1}{3}m_s}{k}}$$

Compound (Physical) Pendulum & Conjugate Centers:

A real physical pendulum (like a baseball bat or flat disc) is simply an extended body swinging about a pivot. Its time period depends on the pivot distance $d$ from the Center of Mass:

$$T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{I_{pivot}}{Mgd}} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{L_{eq}}{g}}$$

where $L_{eq} = d + \frac{k_{cm}^2}{d}$ is the length of an equivalent simple pendulum that swings at the exact same rate.

  • Fastest Swing (Minimum Period): Oscillation period is minimized when the pivot is placed at the body's radius of gyration: $d = k_{cm}$, giving $T_{\min} = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{2k_{cm}}{g}}$.
  • Center of Oscillation ($S$): If you flip the body upside down and pivot it at distance $L_{eq}$ from $O$, it swings with the exact same time period $T$.

6.1.4 Phasor Representation & Superposition of Harmonic Oscillations

Simple Harmonic Motion can be visualized as the projection of Uniform Circular Motion on a diameter. A rotating vector of length $A$ (amplitude) spinning counterclockwise at angular velocity $\omega$ with initial phase $\phi_0$ is called a phasor:

$$x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi_0) = \text{Re}\left[A e^{i(\omega t + \phi_0)}\right]$$

Superposition of Two Collinear SHMs of Same Frequency:

When two oscillations $x_1(t) = A_1\sin(\omega t + \phi_1)$ and $x_2(t) = A_2\sin(\omega t + \phi_2)$ superimpose along the same axis, vector addition of their phasors yields resultant amplitude $A_R$ and phase $\phi_R$:

$$A_R = \sqrt{A_1^2 + A_2^2 + 2A_1 A_2 \cos(\phi_2 - \phi_1)}$$ $$\tan\phi_R = \frac{A_1\sin\phi_1 + A_2\sin\phi_2}{A_1\cos\phi_1 + A_2\cos\phi_2}$$

Notice that this is formally identical to the vector addition of two forces! If phase difference $\Delta\phi = 0$, constructive interference gives $A_R = A_1 + A_2$. If $\Delta\phi = \pi$, destructive interference gives $A_R = |A_1 - A_2|$.

[TRAP ALERT 6.1] Gravitational Potential at Center of Earth

The Trap: Setting $V = 0$ at the center of Earth because gravitational field is zero ($E(0) = 0$).

Definitive Rule: Field is the derivative of potential ($E = -dV/dr$). Zero field means potential has a local extremum, NOT that potential is zero! Potential at the center is the absolute minimum across the universe: $V(0) = \mathbf{-\frac{3}{2}\frac{GM}{R}} = -1.5\frac{GM}{R}$.
[TRAP ALERT 6.2] The Satellite Thrusting Paradox (Orbital Deceleration)

The Trap: A satellite in circular orbit fires forward thrusters to increase its speed.

Definitive Rule: Forward thrust adds energy ($E$ increases, becoming less negative), which raises the satellite into a higher orbit ($r$ increases). But orbital speed is $v = \sqrt{GM/r}$, so in the higher orbit the satellite travels SLOWER! Conversely, firing reverse brakes causes the satellite to drop to a lower orbit where it speeds up!
[TRAP ALERT 6.3] Escape Velocity Angle Independence

The Trap: Believing escape velocity $v_{esc} = \sqrt{2gR}$ applies only to projectiles launched vertically upward.

Definitive Rule: Escape velocity is derived purely from scalar energy conservation ($E \ge 0 \implies \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{GMm}{R} \ge 0$). It is strictly independent of launch direction, provided the trajectory does not intersect the physical surface of the planet!
[TRAP ALERT 6.4] Spring Cut in Length Ratio $m:n$

The Trap: A spring of stiffness $k$ is cut in ratio $1:2$. Students guess the shorter piece has stiffness $k/2$.

Definitive Rule: Spring stiffness is inversely proportional to unstretched length: $k \cdot L = \text{constant}$. For ratio $m:n$, lengths are $L_1 = \frac{m}{m+n}L$ and $L_2 = \frac{n}{m+n}L$. The stiffnesses are: $k_1 = \mathbf{\frac{m+n}{m}k}$ and $k_2 = \mathbf{\frac{m+n}{n}k}$. For ratio $1:2$, $k_1 = 3k$ and $k_2 = \frac{3}{2}k$! Shorter springs are much stiffer.
[TRAP ALERT 6.5] Compound Pendulum Pivot at Center of Mass

The Trap: Moving the pivot point closer to the Center of Mass ($d \to 0$) to minimize the oscillation period.

Definitive Rule: As $d \to 0$, restoring torque $\tau = Mgd\sin\theta \to 0$. Consequently, the time period blows up to infinity: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{k^2+d^2}{gd}} \to \mathbf{\infty}$! The minimum time period occurs at $d = k_{cm}$, NOT at $d = 0$.
[TRAP ALERT 6.6] Instantaneous Mass Addition to an Oscillating Cart

The Trap: Dropping a piece of putty onto a block executing SHM at its extreme amplitude position ($x = A$), and using momentum conservation to decrease the amplitude.

Definitive Rule: At $x = A$, velocity is momentarily zero ($v = 0$). Momentum is trivially zero. The added mass acquires zero velocity, so total mechanical energy remains $E = \frac{1}{2}kA^2$. The amplitude remains completely unchanged ($A' = A$)! (Only the frequency drops to $\omega' = \sqrt{k/(M+m)}$). In contrast, dropping mass at the mean position ($x=0$) conserves momentum and reduces amplitude!
[TRAP ALERT 6.7] Tunnel Through Earth Along an Arbitrary Chord

The Trap: Assuming a tunnel dug along an off-center chord through Earth has a different oscillation period than a diameter tunnel.

Definitive Rule: Gravitational force at distance $r$ is $\mathbf{F} = -\frac{GMm}{R^3}\mathbf{r}$. The component along the chord at displacement $x$ is $F_x = -\frac{GMm}{R^3}x$. The normal force from the tunnel wall balances perpendicular components. The resulting motion is pure SHM with time period $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{R^3}{GM}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{R}{g}} \approx \mathbf{84.6\text{ minutes}}$, strictly identical for ANY chord regardless of distance from center!
[TRAP ALERT 6.8] Two-Block Spring Oscillator Frequency with Moving Wall

The Trap: Applying $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ to a block connected by a spring to another freely sliding block on a smooth floor.

Definitive Rule: When both blocks are free, the Center of Mass is unaccelerated. You MUST use the reduced mass: $\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{\mu}} = \sqrt{\frac{k(m_1 + m_2)}{m_1 m_2}} > \sqrt{\frac{k}{m_1}}$. The frequency is strictly higher than if either block were fixed to a rigid wall.
[TRAP ALERT 6.9] Satellite Total Energy Sign Blunder

The Trap: Writing satellite total energy as positive because kinetic energy is positive.

Definitive Rule: For any bound gravitational orbit, potential energy is negative and twice the magnitude of kinetic energy: $U = -2K$. Therefore, total mechanical energy is strictly negative: $E = K + U = K - 2K = -K = -\frac{GMm}{2r} < 0$. If $E \ge 0$, the orbit is unbounded (parabolic or hyperbolic) and the satellite escapes!
[TRAP ALERT 6.10] Effective 'g' in Liquid Buoyancy Oscillations

The Trap: Finding time period of a floating vertical cylinder pressed down into liquid by writing $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ where $L$ is submerged depth.

Definitive Rule: Restoring force is excess buoyancy: $F = -(\rho A x) g$. Mass of cylinder is $M = \rho_{cyl} A H = \rho A L_{sub}$. Equation: $(\rho A L_{sub}) \ddot{x} + (\rho A g) x = 0 \implies \ddot{x} + \frac{g}{L_{sub}} x = 0$. [WORKED JEE ADVANCED PYQ] JEE Advanced 2017 • Paper 2 Difficulty: Hard • Off-Center Earth Tunnel SHM
A straight, frictionless tunnel is drilled through the Earth along a chord whose perpendicular distance from the Earth's center is $d = R/2$, where $R = 6400\text{ km}$ is the radius of the Earth. (b) Calculate the time taken to travel from one entrance to the other. Take $g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$.
First-Principles Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Gravitational Force at Arbitrary Position along the Chord:

Let $x$ be the displacement of the particle from the midpoint of the tunnel (chord). The radial distance from Earth's center is $r = \sqrt{x^2 + d^2}$.

Inside uniform Earth, gravitational field is linear: $\mathbf{E}(r) = -\frac{GM}{R^3} \mathbf{r} = -g \frac{\mathbf{r}}{R}$.

The gravitational force on particle of mass $m$ is:

$$\mathbf{F}_g = -m \frac{g}{R} \mathbf{r}$$

Step 2: Component Along the Tunnel Axis:

Resolving $\mathbf{F}_g$ along the tunnel axis (the $x$-direction):

$$F_x = \mathbf{F}_g \cdot \hat{\mathbf{i}} = -m \frac{g}{R} (\mathbf{r} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{i}}) = -m \frac{g}{R} x$$

The perpendicular force component $F_y = -m\frac{g}{R}d$ is completely balanced by the normal reaction from the frictionless tunnel wall: $N = m\frac{g}{R}d$.

Step 3: Equation of Motion & Proven SHM:

$$m \ddot{x} = -m \frac{g}{R} x \implies \ddot{x} + \left(\frac{g}{R}\right) x = 0$$

This is precisely the Simple Harmonic Motion differential equation with natural frequency:

$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{R}}$$

Remarkably, $\omega$ is completely independent of the chord distance $d$!

Step 4: Travel Time Between Entrances:

The particle moves from one extreme $x = +x_0$ to the opposite extreme $x = -x_0$, which corresponds to exactly half an oscillation period:

$$t_{travel} = \frac{T}{2} = \frac{\pi}{\omega} = \pi \sqrt{\frac{R}{g}}$$ $$t_{travel} = \pi \sqrt{\frac{6.4 \times 10^6}{9.8}} \approx 3.1416 \times \sqrt{653061} \approx 3.1416 \times 808.12 \approx \mathbf{2538.8\text{ seconds}} \approx \mathbf{42.3\text{ minutes}}$$
